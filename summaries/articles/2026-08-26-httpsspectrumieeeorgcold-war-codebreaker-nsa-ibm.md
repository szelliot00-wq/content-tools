# Secret Cold War IBM Supercomputer Was Built for One Job

Source: https://spectrum.ieee.org/cold-war-codebreaker-nsa-ibm

## Summary
IBM's Harvest (IBM 7950) was a top-secret supercomputer built for the NSA that operated from 1962 to 1976, running up to 200 times faster than any other computer of its era. Designed as a streaming coprocessor bolted onto IBM's Stretch mainframe, it was purpose-built for cryptanalytic work — processing massive volumes of intercepted signals intelligence around the clock during the Cold War's most critical years. It also pioneered the world's first automated robotic tape library (Tractor) and a custom programming language (Alpha) for code-breaking tasks. Though classified and largely unknown, Harvest's innovations foreshadowed technologies that became industry standards decades later.

## Key takeaways
- Harvest ran continuously for 14 years, processing NSA intercepts through the Cuban Missile Crisis, the Vietnam War, and beyond — shut down only when an irreplaceable mechanical part failed.
- Its streaming coprocessor design — a specialized unit attached to a general-purpose CPU — is a direct conceptual ancestor of modern GPUs used for AI and gaming workloads.
- The Tractor automated tape library held 44 GB of simultaneously accessible data in 1962, over 190 times the capacity of IBM's commercial disk storage system announced three years later, and prefigured the robotic storage silos that became enterprise data center standards in the 1980s.
- IBM wrote a custom programming language, Alpha, for Harvest that introduced concepts like wildcard characters and unknown-value propagation years before Unix and modern computing adopted similar ideas.
- Key Harvest designers — Frances Allen (Turing Award 2006), Frederick Brooks Jr. (Turing Award 1999), and James Pomerene — went on to become foundational figures in computing history.
- Harvest's secrecy prevented it from founding a direct lineage of successors, but its pipeline architecture, pattern-matching logic, and automated storage concepts independently resurfaced across the industry in the following decades.
- It was replaced by the Cray-1 in 1976, which won out not because it was better at text processing but because its general-purpose design made it commercially viable for many customers — unlike Harvest, which existed for one customer with one mission.
