# The Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem

Source: https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/8/on-navier-stokes/

## Summary
OpenAI used an unreleased model to resolve the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem, one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems carrying a $1,000,000 award. However, the achievement is mired in controversy: NYU math professor Tristan Buckmaster and Anthropic employee Levent Alpöge had been working on the same problem for nearly a year using OpenAI's own tools (Claude and Codex), and claim OpenAI launched their effort only after rumors of their breakthrough circulated. OpenAI completed their solution in ~88 hours using agents that sent 2.7 million messages and ~130 billion output tokens, then offered Tristan co-authorship but explicitly excluded Levent due to his employment at a competitor.

## Key takeaways
- OpenAI's agents resolved the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem in roughly 88 hours, with Lean formalization taking an additional 17 hours via GPT-6 Astra.
- The solve consumed ~130 billion output tokens for Navier–Stokes alone (~300B total across all attempted problems), which at public API prices would cost around $15 million.
- Buckmaster and Alpöge allege OpenAI launched their effort only *after* rumors of the Anthropic/NYU team's work leaked — raising serious questions about research ethics and priority.
- OpenAI denied accessing the team's Codex session data but did not directly answer whether that data was used in model training.
- The episode illustrates a new dynamic: merely knowing an unpublished solution exists may be enough to trigger massive AI spending to independently "discover" it first.
- Levent Alpöge was excluded from co-authorship solely because he works for Anthropic, OpenAI's competitor — a decision that drew criticism.
- The author draws a parallel to cybersecurity, where a rumor of a vulnerability is now sufficient to set agents loose to find the exploit independently.
