# The foolishness of "natural language programming" (1978)

Source: https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~EWD/transcriptions/EWD06xx/EWD667.html

## Summary
Dijkstra's 1978 essay (EWD 667) argues against the idea of programming computers in natural language. He contends that the shift from machine code to high-level languages was valuable precisely because it introduced formal discipline and redundancy that catches errors — not because it made programming more "casual." Natural language, by contrast, excels at obscuring nonsense, which is the opposite of what programming requires.

## Key takeaways
- Formal symbols in programming are a privilege, not a burden — they enable precision that natural language cannot reliably provide.
- High-level languages improved software safety by making errors detectable; some people mistakenly equated "ease of programming" with the ease of making undetected mistakes.
- Natural language is well-suited for ambiguity and nonsense; programming demands the opposite — exactness and unambiguous instruction.
- The "naturalness" of native tongues is really just familiarity with making statements whose nonsense isn't obvious, a dangerous property in a programming context.
- Dijkstra observed a contemporary decline in written language mastery ("The New Illiteracy"), suggesting that even natural language fluency was eroding — undermining the premise that NL programming would be easier.
- His closing intuition: machines programmable in natural language would be as difficult to build as they would be to use reliably.
