# Why are European countries moving their gold out of North America?

Source: https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cvgyn8q8gqxo

## Summary
European countries, including the Netherlands and France, are repatriating gold reserves from North American vaults, driven by geopolitical instability, trade tensions, and a desire to keep assets more accessible in a crisis. The Netherlands recently moved 86 tonnes from the US and Canada to London, citing the need to be "better prepared for severe crises." London's Bank of England has emerged as the preferred destination due to its role as a major global gold trading hub. Experts note the trend reflects broader strategic thinking around reserve asset management rather than fear of imminent economic collapse.

## Key takeaways
- The Netherlands moved 86 tonnes of gold from North America to London (March–August 2026); France also repatriated its US reserves; Germany completed a similar transfer by 2016.
- London is the preferred destination because of its role as the world's top gold trading centre, making assets quickly accessible in a crisis.
- Geopolitical and trade tensions are a factor, but experts say gold relocation is also driven by inflation hedging, interest rate concerns, and better reserve management strategy.
- Central banks globally have roughly doubled their gold buying pace — averaging 1,000 tonnes/year over the past four years vs. 500 tonnes/year previously.
- Gold hit a record high of ~$5,394/oz in February 2026 before pulling back to ~$4,046/oz in July 2026; Goldman Sachs forecasts $4,900/oz by end of 2026.
- Physically moving gold is complex and expensive; many transfers are handled via same-day buy/sell transactions rather than physical shipment, with firms like Brink's reporting increased demand for actual physical transfers.
- Storing gold domestically carries significant costs (security, insurance, auditing), which is why smaller nations often prefer established custodians like the Bank of England.
