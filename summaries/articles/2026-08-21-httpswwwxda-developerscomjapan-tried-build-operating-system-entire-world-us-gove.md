# Japan tried to build an operating system for the world, the US intervened

Source: https://www.xda-developers.com/japan-tried-build-operating-system-entire-world-us-government-intervened/

## Summary
Japan's TRON Project, launched in 1984 by Ken Sakamura at the University of Tokyo, was an ambitious effort to build a vertically integrated computing platform — from embedded microcontrollers to desktop OSes — entirely on Japanese architecture. Its desktop variant, BTRON, featured a radical hypermedia document model decades ahead of its time, but was effectively killed after the US Trade Representative named it in a 1989 trade barrier report. The embedded variant, ITRON, survived and quietly became one of the most widely deployed operating systems in history, running in an estimated 60% of the world's embedded devices. SoftBank founder Masayoshi Son has been accused — including by Sakamura himself — of lobbying to sink BTRON to protect his American software import business.

## Key takeaways
- TRON was a government-backed Japanese initiative comprising five sub-architectures covering everything from embedded systems (ITRON) to desktop PCs (BTRON) to telecom switches (CTRON), with its own CPU, keyboard layout, and character encoding.
- BTRON's document model — where the fundamental unit was a typed, linkable content part rather than a file — anticipated concepts that tools like Obsidian and Roam are rediscovering 40 years later.
- TRON Code supported up to 1.5 million characters and exceeded Unicode's CJK coverage for over a decade, with Braille included as a first-class plane from the start.
- The 1989 US Trade Representative report named BTRON as a trade barrier over Japanese government procurement steering, but no sanctions were ever actually applied — the reputational damage alone was enough to cause Japanese manufacturers to abandon it.
- Masayoshi Son, whose business depended on importing US PC software, allegedly lobbied against BTRON and may have exploited the USTR report to damage it; Sakamura directly names him on the TRON Project's 30th anniversary site.
- ITRON succeeded precisely because it was small, royalty-free, and ignored by the political spotlight — it shipped in digital cameras, car ECUs, mobile phones, and home appliances, and was adopted as IEEE standard 2050-2018 in 2018.
- The project illustrates how the same openness that made TRON politically vulnerable (no IP wall to hide behind) was what allowed ITRON to spread freely into billions of devices.
