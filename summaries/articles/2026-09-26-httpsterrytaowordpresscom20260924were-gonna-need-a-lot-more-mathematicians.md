# We're gonna need a lot more mathematicians

Source: https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/09/24/were-gonna-need-a-lot-more-mathematicians/

## Summary
Written by cryptographer Amit Sahai and posted on Terence Tao's blog, this piece argues that as AI systems produce increasingly sophisticated mathematical ideas, humanity must resist the temptation to cede understanding entirely to machines. Using the example of an AI-designed fusion power plant, Sahai contends that human communities must be capable of independently verifying and comprehending consequential AI-generated breakthroughs. Rather than seeing AI's speed as a reason to abandon mathematical research, he calls for a significant expansion of mathematically sophisticated researchers who can serve as a "deployable intellectual reserve" for society.

## Key takeaways
- AI is already generating genuinely novel mathematical ideas, not just performing calculations, and this will accelerate — making all researchers experience the humility of not being able to keep up.
- Delegating understanding entirely to AI is a serious risk: when AI proposes high-stakes designs (e.g., a fusion plant), human communities must be able to independently assess safety and correctness, not just trust the machine.
- A theorem's guarantee is only as good as the model it rests in — understanding the model, its assumptions, and its uncertainties requires humans with deep mathematical sophistication.
- Society needs a "deployable intellectual reserve" of mathematically trained people who can be called upon to understand AI-enabled breakthroughs, even outside their usual research areas.
- This requires broadening what mathematicians see as their vocation — embracing the work of comprehending AI-generated results as a first-class contribution, not a lesser substitute for proving theorems.
- Individual human capacity is biologically limited, so the only path forward is expanding the *number* of mathematicians working collaboratively, not relying on a few exceptional people to work faster.
- Independent expertise is critical: if only the proposing organization's experts can follow a technical argument, meaningful public oversight becomes impossible.
