# If math is more than proof, we need to better celebrate the rest of it

Source: https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/09/18/if-math-is-more-than-proof-we-need-to-better-celebrate-the-rest-of-it/

## Summary
This guest post by Grant Sanderson (of 3Blue1Brown) on Terence Tao's blog argues that as AI systems increasingly generate mathematical proofs, the math community needs to formally recognize and reward "motivated explanations" — work that answers "how would you think of that?" rather than just establishing what is true. Sanderson draws on examples from Bill Thurston, Timothy Gowers, and the Princeton Companion to Mathematics to show that such explanatory work already exists but is treated as a second-class activity. He proposes concrete institutional changes to elevate its status, framing this shift as both intellectually urgent and strategically important for the field's public image.

## Key takeaways
- Proofs have historically been proxies for mathematical understanding, but AI-generated proofs decouple the two — the community needs new proxies that more directly measure human understanding.
- A "motivated explanation" answers why a theorem is the right one to pose, where each idea comes from, and how it fits its surrounding context — not just why it is true. Unlike proofs, validity is not binary, but motivatedness is still meaningfully verifiable.
- Exemplary motivated explanations already exist (Princeton Companion to Mathematics, Thurston's essay "On Proof and Progress", Chow's "A beginner's guide to forcing"), but their authors often needed prior prestige (e.g. a Fields Medal) to justify spending time on them.
- "Open exposition problems" — important results that lack clear, motivated explanations — should be treated with the same seriousness as open research problems, especially as AI-generated proofs multiply.
- Practical proposals include: PhD deliverables as talks rather than write-ups, a Hilbert-style list of unsolved exposition problems, journals focused on exposition, and tenure/hiring credit for great textbooks and expository writing.
- Visibly shifting the field's culture now would reassure young mathematicians that their career value doesn't depend on out-proving machines, and would strengthen the case for funding mathematics as a public good.
