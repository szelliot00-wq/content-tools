# Of Gods and Languages: On "When God Spoke Greek" (2013)

Source: https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/of-gods-and-languages-on-when-god-spoke-greek/

## Summary
Kevin Hart reviews Timothy Michael Law's *When God Spoke Greek* (2013), a book tracing the history and significance of the Septuagint — the Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures used by early Christians as their Bible. Law argues that the Septuagint is not merely a translation but a distinct textual tradition that preserves older versions of scripture, sometimes differing theologically from the received Hebrew Bible. The discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls in 1947 reinforced this, showing that older Hebrew manuscripts often aligned more closely with the Septuagint than with the later standardized Hebrew text.

## Key takeaways
- The early Christian Church used a Greek Bible — the Septuagint (LXX) — for both Old and New Testaments, not the Hebrew text most modern Bibles are based on.
- The Septuagint originated in Alexandria around the 3rd century BCE, beginning with the Torah, and expanded over time; it was considered a divine revelation, not merely a translation.
- The Dead Sea Scrolls revealed that older Hebrew manuscripts frequently aligned with the Septuagint, suggesting the received Hebrew Bible in places reflects later revisions of earlier texts.
- Jerome's Vulgate (Latin Bible), which displaced the Septuagint-based *Vetus Latina*, shifted Christianity toward Hebrew-based scriptural authority — a legacy that largely persists today.
- The Septuagint sometimes differs theologically from the Hebrew: for example, depicting God as a peacemaker rather than a warrior (Exodus 15:3), and Paul selectively quoted the LXX to support his theology of universal salvation.
- Law's book is notable as the first accessible introduction to Septuagint studies for non-specialists, aided by the 2007 *New English Translation of the Septuagint* (NETS).
