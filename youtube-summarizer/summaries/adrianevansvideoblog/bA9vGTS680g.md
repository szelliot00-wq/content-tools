# From SAS soldier to Psychologist: Harry Moffitt on Leadership, Intuition and the Fourth Pillar

Video ID: `bA9vGTS680g`

## Summary
Former Australian SAS soldier turned psychologist Harry Moffitt joins host Adrian to discuss leadership, high performance, and human flourishing. Drawing on his 30-year military career and subsequent psychology practice, Moffitt shares insights from his book *The Fourth Pillar*, arguing that stoicism, intuition, reflective practice, and a philosophical disposition are underrated foundations of sustained elite performance. The conversation weaves together military experience, sports psychology, corporate leadership, and ancient philosophy to build a case for staying grounded in basics, community, and self-knowledge.

## Key insights
- **Community and roots as a performance anchor.** Moffitt credits staying connected to local community — mates, the local pub, old friends — as a constant throughout every phase of his career. Business leaders who disconnect from that base risk losing a stabilizing sense of identity.
- **Stoicism is misread as emotional suppression.** True stoicism is about moderation, virtue, and disciplined attention — not the "stiff upper lip" performance of hiding feelings. Emotions are ancient intelligences; the goal is to understand and work with them, not suppress them.
- **The "golden mean" under uncertainty.** The best decision-makers under incomplete information sit between paralysis-by-analysis and reckless impulsiveness. An 80% information solution acted on now usually beats waiting for perfect clarity that never arrives.
- **Intuition is earned pattern recognition, not magic.** Gut feel is the compressed output of thousands of hours of experience and reflective practice. It deserves rehabilitation — the data-science backlash against intuition (Kahneman et al.) understates how much expertise lives in unconscious pattern recognition.
- **Reflective practice is an epidemic absence in business.** After-action reviews are standard in elite military and medical settings but rare in corporate life. Even a quick "what did we intend / what happened / what next" after a deal or project phase builds the experiential data set that feeds intuition over time.
- **Brilliant at the basics outlasts brilliant.** The most satisfied, longest-lasting performers — Moffitt's example is AFL player Scott Pendlebury — aren't necessarily the most gifted. They master fundamentals, ignore distraction, and tend to their whole life (family, rest, community), not just their craft.
- **The fourth pillar: a philosophical condition.** Alongside physical, mental, and social wellbeing, Moffitt proposes a philosophical condition — the habit of examining life, approaching problems from multiple angles, and sitting with uncertainty. It's what lets you "view the whole mountain" rather than just traverse it one way.
- **Transitions require a broader identity.** Veterans, retiring executives, and athletes who let their professional role become their entire self face a hard landing. The practical fix: invest continuously in friendships, hobbies, and community so there's something to return to when the role ends.
- **The four things humans need.** Care (knowing someone gives a damn about you), validation (feedback on your work and purpose), security (psychological and financial), and growth (being stretched toward your elastic limit). Leaders who attend to all four get discretionary effort; those who ignore them get compliance at best.
- **Eight daily practices worth protecting.** Learning, practicing, waiting, watching, listening, talking, reading, writing, thinking, and walking — Moffitt's "eight privileges." Modernity is eroding all of them; the simple act of walking is down roughly 90% and is one of the most cognitively complex things humans do.
- **Humor and play are non-negotiable.** A sense of humor, music, dance, and shared communal experiences (concerts, even churches) are places of goodwill that recharge the human system. Taking offense too easily is itself a performance liability.
- **Morning routine as an automation of the base.** A consistent, boring morning routine frees cognitive bandwidth for creative and intuitive thinking. The goal is to automate the mundane so the mind can surface the insights it was working on overnight.
