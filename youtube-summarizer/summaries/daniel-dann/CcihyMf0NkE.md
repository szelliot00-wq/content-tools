## Summary
Daniel tests five AI detection tools (Proofademic, GPTZero, Copyleaks, Scribbr, and ZeroGPT) against three identical student essays — one human-written, one ChatGPT-generated, and one hybrid — scoring each on accuracy, false positive protection, and quality of classroom-facing explanations. ## Key insights
- **False positives are the central risk for teachers.** Four of five tools (GPTZero, Copyleaks, Scribbr, ZeroGPT) flagged the entirely human-written essay as AI-generated, which could trigger unfair academic misconduct conversations.
- **GPTZero produced contradictory results:** it classified the human essay as 100% AI, yet classified the pure ChatGPT essay as 99% mixed/human — essentially inverting the correct answers on both.
- **Copyleaks gave identical scores to both the human and AI essays** (100% AI for both), making it unable to distinguish between them despite offering a detailed structural scan.
- **Scribbr gave the human essay a 65% human score** (a false positive) and correctly flagged the ChatGPT essay at 100%, but failed to provide sentence-level breakdowns on the mixed essay — only vague overall percentages.
- **ZeroGPT flagged ~97% of the human essay as AI**, then gave the ChatGPT essay ~97% AI and the mixed essay ~98% AI — essentially producing the same high score regardless of actual content, and highlighting random human sentences while missing real AI blocks.
- **Proofademic was the only tool to correctly classify all three essays:** 81% human for the human essay, 99% AI for the ChatGPT essay, and 62% AI for the mixed essay.
- **On the mixed essay, Proofademic identified 5 human sentences, 8 AI sentences, and 2 mixed sentences**, pinpointing exactly where AI-generated content began.
- **Color-coded sentence highlighting** (green = human, orange = AI, yellow = mixed/uncertain) is presented as a critical feature for teachers who need visual, defensible evidence.
- **Proofademic additional features:** built-in plagiarism checker, support for 23 languages, batch scanning, and downloadable PDF reports for entire classrooms.

## Patterns & frameworks

**Three-Essay Benchmark Protocol**
A repeatable testing method: run the same three essays — (1) entirely human-written, (2) entirely AI-generated, (3) a hybrid combining both — through each tool under identical conditions. This isolates three distinct failure modes: false positives on human work, missed AI detection, and inability to distinguish mixed content.

**Three Scoring Dimensions**
Each tool is evaluated on: (1) *Accuracy* — does it correctly classify each essay type? (2) *False positive protection* — does it avoid wrongly flagging human writing? (3) *Actionable classroom explanations* — does it provide evidence a teacher can actually use and communicate to students and parents?

**Sentence-Level Visual Evidence Standard**
The video frames a "best practice" expectation: a good academic AI detector should not just return a percentage but should highlight individual sentences with color coding (human vs. AI vs. uncertain), giving teachers a navigable roadmap rather than a single number to defend.

**Percentage-Alone Warning**
An implicit mental model runs throughout: a single aggregate percentage score is insufficient and potentially dangerous in an academic context. The right interpretation layer requires granular breakdown, which most mainstream free tools do not provide.
