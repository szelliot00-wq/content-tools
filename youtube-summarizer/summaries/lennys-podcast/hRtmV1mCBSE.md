# Why “everyone’s a builder” isn’t working | A reality check with Sherif Mansour

Video ID: `hRtmV1mCBSE`

## Summary
Sherif Mansour, Head of AI and Product Craft at Atlassian, challenges popular narratives about product management in the AI era — specifically the idea that "everyone's a builder" makes PMs obsolete. Through a live Atlassian experiment and years of PM coaching, he argues that AI increases throughput but creates a greater need for direction, context-sharing, and specialized craft. The conversation covers PM archetypes, hiring trends, career development, and nuanced takes on common product maxims. It is most relevant to PMs, product leaders, and anyone thinking about how AI changes team structure and product roles.

---

## Key insights

- **PMs provide "decision-making velocity," not decisions.** Their core job is filling context gaps — customer context, product/market context, and business context — so the team can make better decisions independently, not waiting on the PM to approve every move.

- **The "everyone's a builder" experiment failed at Atlassian.** They created squads with no PMs or designers, only AI builders from various crafts. Within weeks, teams reverted to traditional roles. The conclusion: throughput is cheap; direction is the scarce resource. The internal motto became "direct the ship, don't row it."

- **AI amplifies strengths and weaknesses equally.** Just as the Balsamiq mockup era showed that giving everyone a design tool didn't make everyone a good designer, AI prototyping doesn't make everyone a good product thinker. Specialization still matters; "you can only get the AI to do something as good as your knowledge of the specialization."

- **PM roles are the fastest-growing in tech right now.** Per an unpublished state-of-the-tech-job-market report referenced in the episode: PM roles are surging, engineering is slowing, design is flat, and user research globally has only ~108 open roles. This is the opposite of what many predicted when AI arrived.

- **Artist-type PMs are trending up in value** because when everyone has access to the same AI tools and intelligence, taste and experience become the differentiator. Sherif predicts this is also true for designers, though the market hasn't caught up yet — his hypothesis is that design will become more important than ever as experience becomes a key competitive moat.

- **Juniors are underrated in the AI era.** Many orgs are incorrectly deprioritizing junior hires. Juniors bring AI-native ways of working and are ~38% more likely to adopt new tools than seniors. Seniors provide domain expertise to catch AI slop. The ideal is a complementary team: juniors generate and explore, seniors evaluate quality.

- **Starting with the solution is sometimes the right move.** Two legitimate cases: (1) giving customers a canvas (like Rovo Chat) to discover what problems to productize, and (2) product strategy, where you start with the assets you have and reason outward. Amazon's "everything store" logic — starting from "what else needs a massive inventory?" — is the cited example.

- **"Bolting AI onto" existing workflows is actually a valid strategy for established products.** Rather than rebuilding from scratch, Atlassian mapped existing human workflows in Jira and added agent hooks to them. The key formula: identify human workflows → find where agents can add value in steps → measure and iterate.

- **The PM craft triangle framework** (artist, scientist, general manager) helps map the right PM to the right work. Mismatches cause PMs to struggle not because they're bad, but because the work type doesn't align with their natural discovery style.

- **Shared brain-building is the core scalable PM behavior.** Examples: never do customer interviews without engineers in the room, get engineers to man customer booths at events, have the whole team try different AI tools and report back. The goal is distributed empathy and shared context, not centralized knowledge.

- **New managers go hands-off too quickly.** The best managers lead by example, get into the work with the team, and help navigate outcomes together. Creating space is good; disappearing entirely is not.

- **Individual contributor (IC) paths to exec-level are underused.** Many PMs assume they need to manage to get strategic influence. Atlassian has distinguished PMs who contribute at the exec level as ICs. Sherif advocates loudly for dual-track career paths.

- **On working with visionary founders/execs:** assume they're right and you're wrong until you've caught up to their chapter. Your job is to close the context gap between where they are and where the team is — not to follow orders blindly or dismiss their view as a HIPPO.

## Use cases

- **PM teams restructuring around AI:** Use the "direct the ship" framing to argue against converting PM/designer roles into generic AI builder roles.
- **Hiring managers building PM teams:** Apply the artist/scientist/general manager triangle to match PM type to team needs rather than treating all PM hires as interchangeable.
- **Senior PMs stagnating:** Diagnose whether you're in the wrong type of work, not necessarily performing poorly — a team swap may unlock energy and results.
- **Early-career PMs seeking acceleration:** Look for projects that match your natural strengths; signal to your manager where you want to grow using a framework like the triangle.
- **Organizations unsure whether to hire juniors:** Use the complementary team model — juniors for adoption and generation, seniors for quality evaluation and slop detection.
- **Product teams deciding whether to rebuild vs. evolve with AI:** Use the "map existing human workflows → bolt on agents → productize high-value use cases" approach rather than greenfield rewrites.
- **PMs working with strong-opinion execs:** Use the flash-tag / conviction-clarifying technique to distinguish FYI input from binding decisions, and represent both sides of a decision to the team.
- **Organizations wanting to scale product knowledge:** Apply the shared-brain approach — co-create with engineers, include them in customer interviews, run AI builder weeks across all crafts simultaneously.

## Patterns & frameworks

**PM Craft Triangle (Geoff Redfern / Atlassian)**
Three archetypes of product manager, defined by how they naturally approach product discovery:
- *Scientist*: hypothesis-driven, AB testing, growth metrics, data-driven
- *General Manager*: TAM-focused, revenue/business model oriented, analytical
- *Artist*: vision-led, experience-first, emotionally intuitive, starts with solutions and works backward

Use it in hiring, calibration, and career coaching. Most people sit closer to one vertex. Those who span all three are rare and valuable mainly in zero-to-one or startup contexts.

**Three Types of Context (Sherif Mansour)**
PMs should constantly fill gaps across three dimensions:
1. Customer context — who the user is, what they need, what they struggle with
2. Product and market context — competitive landscape, strategic positioning
3. Business context — operating model, revenue, constraints

The PM's job is to identify where the gap is and choose activities (interviews, workshops, shared sessions) to close it.

**Direct the Ship, Don't Row It**
An internal Atlassian principle. As AI makes throughput cheaper, the scarce resource becomes direction. PMs should focus on steering — vision, alignment, context — not on being the fastest builder.

**Human Workflow → Agent Augmentation Pattern**
For established products: (1) map existing human workflows, (2) identify steps where agents can add value, (3) allow agents in loops on those steps, (4) measure outcomes, (5) productize high-use-case patterns as native features. Used in Jira agent loops and Confluence whiteboard sticky-note clustering.

**Canvas-First Discovery**
Give users an open-ended tool (a "canvas") to solve their own problems, then observe what they build. Use those manual workarounds to identify what to productize. Called "paving the cow path." Applied at Atlassian via Rovo Chat and agent creation tools.

**Flash Tags (Dharmesh Shah, referenced)**
A communication protocol for leaders sharing opinions: tag each statement with its intent — #FYI (awareness only), suggestion, recommendation, or plea (strong request). Prevents teams from treating every exec opinion as a directive. Sherif's addition: when executives don't tag themselves, it's the PM's job to ask the clarifying question about conviction level and decision ownership.

**Complementary Team Model for AI Era**
Pair juniors (high tool-adoption rate, AI-native, generative) with seniors (domain expertise, slop detection, quality evaluation). Neither group alone is sufficient. Observed empirically during Atlassian's internal AI builder weeks.
