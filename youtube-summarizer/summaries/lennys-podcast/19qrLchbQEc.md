# An 86-minute guide to finding PMF | Rob Snyder (Author, The Power of Pull)

Video ID: `19qrLchbQEc`

## Summary
Rob Snyder, author of *The Power of Pull*, presents a pragmatic framework for finding product-market fit at the zero-to-one stage of a startup. The core argument is that founders fail not because their product lacks value, but because they target people who aren't "coiled springs" — people who are actively stuck on a specific project and can't complete it with existing options. The PULL acronym defines exactly what that person looks like, and the entire go-to-market process flows from finding and validating that person before building anything. The advice is most relevant to early-stage B2B founders, product leaders at larger companies launching new products, and anyone who has spent years pushing a product nobody urgently needs.

---

## Key insights

- **Pull is not about pain points or value props.** Founders get trapped believing that identifying a painful problem and offering a differentiated solution is enough. It isn't. People can acknowledge your product solves real problems and still not drop their priorities to buy. Pull requires a person who is *actively trying to do something right now* and is *blocked*.

- **Pull is a property of a specific person, not a company.** You don't sell to SpaceX — you sell to the head of legal at SpaceX who has a project on her to-do list she can't complete with existing tools. Defining your customer as a company or a market segment is a common and costly mistake.

- **The PULL acronym defines the four conditions that must all be present simultaneously:**
  - **P – Project:** A specific job they are actively trying to get done (not a vague category like "save time" — more like "minimize the time I spend inputting notes into our CRM after client calls").
  - **U – Unavoidable:** They have to do it *right now*, not eventually. There is a forcing function — regulatory, deadline-driven, or operationally critical.
  - **L – List of options:** They have already considered alternatives — hiring, existing software, doing it themselves.
  - **L – Limitations:** Those alternatives are not good enough. They are genuinely blocked.

- **The right signal is a "pull-shaped action," not enthusiasm.** Buyers leaning back and saying "wow, that's fascinating, that's the future of our industry" is a trap. The real signal is someone who says "How do we get started? Does the product even exist yet?" or "If you can do that, I will pay you right now." Pupils dilating, leaning forward, urgency — these are the physical signs.

- **Weird not to buy is the standard.** Most people, most of the time, would be *weird to buy* your product given their situation. You are hunting for the rare person for whom it would be *weird not to buy*.

- **Financial advisors / Jump example:** Jump (AI notetaker for financial advisors) illustrates all four PULL elements cleanly. P = minimize note-taking time. U = SEC compliance means notes *must* be taken or they face legal consequences. L = generic AI notetakers exist. LL = those tools don't integrate with their CRM and aren't SEC-compliant. Pull = ripping the product out of your hands. Insurance agents fail the same test because the U is absent — they're not required to log notes, so they just... don't.

- **Don't try to manufacture pull — go find it.** Pull exists independently of you. Your job is not to convince people to want your product; it's to find the people who already have pull. This reframe is liberating: incompetence at convincing = not finding the right person yet.

- **Firsthand immersion is how you find the best pull hypotheses.** One example: a founder with no idea shadowed small businesses industry by industry — solar installers (hated it), physical therapists (loved it) — spending days watching their workflow until he found where they were stuck. He considered *buying* a PT franchise just to see the P&L. The idea emerged from observation, not a whiteboard.

- **Solve your own problem only counts if you would have paid someone else to solve it.** Emily at Clearly AI found something at AWS she genuinely would have paid to solve and no one offered it. That's valid. Building something you think "the world needs" but wouldn't personally buy urgently is not the same thing.

- **Founder outreach should be "interesting, let's chat" — not a pitch.** Cold LinkedIn/email messages should be brief (1–2 sentences), surprising or noteworthy, and focused only on getting a meeting — not convincing anyone to buy. Examples: "Hey, Snoop Dogg invested in us and I thought you'd find it interesting, would you be open to chat?" or "I dropped out of my PhD and I'm now working on something for content marketers." No AI personalization; no value prop; no pain point identification.

- **Benchmark for cold LinkedIn outreach:** 30–40% should accept connection requests; of those, 30–50% should reply; a majority of those should agree to a call. If you're under these numbers, your profile or message isn't working.

- **The sales call has two phases: pull discovery, then conceptual pitch.** Open by asking *why they took the call today* — this often reveals their pull for free. If not, earn the right to ask discovery questions ("Do you mind if I ask a few questions to understand your context better?"). Then pitch in a small, specific way — not a grand vision. "Sounds like you're looking for X without the hassle of Y. Is that roughly it?" — and let them pull more information from you.

- **The Hail Mary question:** When a call doesn't surface pull, ask: "Is there anything on your to-do list you would pay someone to take off your plate right now?" This open-ended pivot has launched multiple startups. One company selling an applicant tracking system into government pivoted to job description writing after this question.

- **Do 5 calls, then assess.** If five calls produce zero pull and zero interesting learning, something is wrong — either the message, the profile, or the hypothesis. Adjust and run again.

- **Goblin mode:** Break-glass-in-case-of-emergency behavior when you're totally stuck. Book a flight to a conference tonight, schedule 20 meetings, have parents go door-to-door to dental offices. Tiro (dental staffing startup) literally did the parents-door-to-door approach. It's unsustainable but can generate a hypothesis when nothing else is working.

- **No design partners, no pilots, no POCs (unless they insist).** Design partners "play Steve Jobs" — they give feedback as if helping you build a product, not as someone who actually needs it done. The people with the most urgent pull don't *want* to be design partners; they just want the thing done. Proactively offering pilots signals that you don't believe in the urgency yourself.

- **After the first yes, use the pull framework to set pricing bounds.** What is the value of completing the project? What do the existing (insufficient) options cost? Both anchor a price frame. Pharma market research example: if consultants charge $50k/year, that's your anchor. For sensitive pricing conversations, present it as "others have done it in a range from $80k to $1M, with most in the middle — how are you thinking about this?" rather than proposing a number directly.

- **Delivery and retention: find the "weird to churn" moment.** Everyone who buys will churn until they hit some specific milestone. Slack's was 10,000 messages. Snyder's restaurant retention product's was: they received an alert about an at-risk employee they were able to save within the first two weeks. Design onboarding to get every customer to that milestone fast. Ask constantly "are you going to churn?" until they say "I can't live without this."

- **The Case Study Factory mental model.** A startup is a machine that repeats one case study (the ideal hell-yes customer) infinitely. The factory has three steps: Pipeline (get people to talk to you), Sales (turn them into customers), Delivery (make them weird to churn). Apply theory of constraints: find the bottleneck, fix it, move to the next one. This simplifies everything and tells you exactly where to focus at any given moment.

- **"Hell yes" customers** are those who were weird not to buy and are weird to churn. They pull the product out of your hands before and after purchase. Your only job once you've found one is to find more who are basically clones.

- **The tarpit trap:** Copying a space where there's already a clear winner, without a specific theory of why a particular customer *is blocked* in a way the current leader doesn't solve, almost never works. "It's better" isn't enough. A 100x better printer is useless if the buyer isn't blocked by their current printer.

- **Applies beyond startups:** At mature companies, the products that worked came out of sales calls where customers said "yes, but could you also do *this* for us?" The ones that came from whiteboards didn't. New product launches at scale should run the same PULL process, ideally led by founders or the most senior product leader.

## Use cases

- **Early-stage B2B founders** stuck in "design partner purgatory" who keep getting encouragement but no closed revenue.
- **Founders pivoting** after 12–18 months without traction, trying to diagnose which of the four PULL conditions is missing.
- **Product leaders at growth-stage companies** launching a second product or new business line who want to validate before building.
- **Solo founders with no warm network** who need a cold outreach strategy that doesn't require a polished pitch deck or product demo.
- **Technical founders** who default to building before selling and need a framework to delay building until there's a validated pull signal.
- **Consumer founders** — while the framework is B2B-native, the Spanx example shows it works for consumer too (Sara Blakely solved her own urgent problem and would have paid someone).
- **Anyone who keeps getting "that's so interesting, send me more info"** responses and wants to understand why that's not a signal of pull.
- **Sales and GTM teams** wanting a structured way to qualify/disqualify deals using the case study filter ("you don't look like our case study — probably not a fit").

## Patterns & frameworks

**1. The PULL Framework (acronym)**
A four-part test to determine whether a specific person will urgently buy your product. All four must be true simultaneously:
- **P**roject: A specific thing on their mental to-do list they're actively trying to accomplish.
- **U**navoidable: There's a forcing function making it urgent *right now*.
- **L**ist of options: They've considered alternatives (DIY, hire someone, existing tools).
- **L**imitations: Those alternatives are not good enough — they are genuinely blocked.

**2. The Pull Hypothesis Slide (2-slide template)**
Slide 1: "Here's what I hear: you're trying to [Project] right now because of [Unavoidable reason], and you've looked at [List of options] but they're lacking because [Limitations]. Do I have that roughly right?"
Slide 2: Conceptual overview — "It sounds like you need [small, specific thing]. We could offer that. Does that fit?"
Use to pressure-test a hypothesis before building anything.

**3. The Hail Mary Question**
When a conversation isn't surfacing pull: "Is there anything on your to-do list you would pay someone to take off your plate right now?"
Purpose: Generate a new hypothesis mid-call instead of ending empty-handed.

**4. Goblin Mode**
Break-glass emergency tactic when you have no pull hypothesis and calls aren't yielding anything. Involves doing whatever it takes to get in front of the maximum number of potential customers as fast as possible — flying to conferences last-minute, door-to-door, booking 20 meetings in a day — to generate raw signal you can then shape into a hypothesis.

**5. The Case Study Factory**
A mental model for how a startup works at scale. One case study (ideal hell-yes customer, from pull to retention) repeated infinitely. Three pipeline stages: Pipeline → Sales → Delivery. Apply theory of constraints to find and fix the bottleneck in the factory at any given time. Useful because it unifies all startup activity under a single repeatable loop rather than dozens of disconnected priorities.

**6. Pull-Shaped Action (buying signal test)**
Instead of looking for enthusiasm, look for *urgency of next steps*: "How do we get started? Does the product exist yet? Can I pay you right now?" Anything that delays — "we'll think about it," "could you send information for our team to review," "maybe Q4" — is a non-signal regardless of how positive the tone.

**7. "Weird Not to Buy" standard**
Flip the default assumption. Most people are weird *to* buy your product given their priorities. You're hunting for the rare person for whom it is weird *not* to buy. Applying this filter instantly clarifies whether a warm conversation is real signal or polite noise.

**8. The Coiled Spring metaphor**
A person with all four PULL conditions active is like a coiled spring — energy already stored, waiting to be released. You don't need to create the energy; you just need to show up at the right moment and remove the block.

**9. Pricing via PULL framework**
Use the L's (list of options and their limitations) to anchor price. What does the best existing alternative cost? What is the value of completing the project? Both create natural price boundaries. Present pricing as a range observed in others rather than a direct proposal to reduce friction.
