# Why the best product leaders are becoming ICs again | Tom Verrilli (CPO of Whatnot)

Video ID: `ruvis-VWg2s`

## Summary
Tom Verrilli, CPO of Whatnot and former long-time CPO of Twitch, argues that product management as a specialist function has been over-deployed across tech, creating bloated org structures that infantilize engineers and designers who are capable of making product decisions themselves. His core thesis — "we regret that product management exists" — is not anti-PM but a forcing function to hire PMs only when there is a specific, justified need rather than filling a headcount ratio. The interview covers how AI is amplifying individual contributor (IC) leverage, why senior PMs should return to hands-on work instead of managing layers, and what traits actually matter in the rare PM worth hiring. It is most relevant to CPOs, VPs of product, senior PMs navigating the current job market, and founders thinking about how to structure their product org.

## Key insights

- **"We regret that product management exists"** is Whatnot's founding principle for its product team — not a statement that PMs are bad, but a deliberate reminder to never hire one by default. Every PM hire requires a specific, justified need.

- **The PM-as-specialist model emerged from scale, not necessity.** Early tech companies had founders talking directly to engineers and designers. As internet businesses scaled faster than any prior industry, delegation became necessary — but the "pod ratio" (1 PM per 6 engineers) was an HR artifact, not an optimal design.

- **Hiring too many PMs infantilizes engineers and designers.** When there is always a PM to make decisions, engineers and designers never develop the "product muscle." Removing PMs forces those muscles to grow, leading to better product decisions from the full team.

- **Whatnot has ~21–22 PMs** supporting a marketplace that is the fastest-growing US marketplace business of all time, by GMV — an extremely lean ratio by industry standards.

- **31,832 people applied to be a PM at Whatnot over 2 years. They hired one.** The pool is large but the proportion with genuine product skills (vs. process/politics skills) is very small.

- **What's trending DOWN in PM interviews at Whatnot:** Candidates who lead with "driving alignment," "stakeholder management," and "keeping leadership informed." This signals a PM whose specialty was organizational politics, not customer understanding or technical depth.

- **What's trending UP:** Candidates who demonstrate both macro thinking (how does the system work as a whole?) and micro impatience (what is the smallest thing I can do to validate this right now?). Specificity about decisions they personally made — not things they "owned" at large companies with inertia.

- **The best PMs are returning to IC work.** The traditional promotion path took A-players and made them directors who spent 100% of their time in alignment meetings and coaching, removing them from doing the actual work. At Whatnot, even managers spend 90%+ of their time as ICs. Tom himself spends ~50% of his time on IC work as CPO.

- **Why IC leadership is more effective:** A senior PM with 15 years of experience can make faster, higher-quality decisions than 3 junior PMs going through review cycles. They also see more of the "board" at once, making intuitively correct cross-system decisions. Example: At Twitch, the discovery team and ads team were perpetually at war over impressions. Tom's fix was to assign one PM accountable for both — the conflict dissolved immediately because the PM naturally optimized for GMV from the feed rather than defending a silo.

- **The Messi analogy:** "Why wouldn't you want Messi playing for your team rather than trying to have the academy coming along all the time?" Senior PMs doing IC work is more valuable than senior PMs coaching junior PMs to eventually reach senior quality.

- **IC work for a PM does not primarily mean shipping code.** It means: being in support tickets, pulling data yourself, sitting with engineering and design, querying the code base directly, writing the spec, and running standups. Tom has personally shipped production code at Whatnot but acknowledges it's not the best use of his time.

- **AI's top three productivity unlocks for PMs:**
  1. **Data science democratization.** Tools like Hex Threads allow PMs to pull nuanced cohorts, build sensitivity models, run regression models, and analyze individual user logs in hours — work that took a week with a dedicated data scientist in 2017. Tom spends 10x more time in data than ever before while spending less time talking to data scientists.
  2. **Code base comprehension without bothering engineers.** PMs can now query the code base via AI tools to understand system logic, LOEs, and how features interact — without distracting engineering velocity.
  3. **Real-time user research + code debugging loop.** On Whatnot's live platform, Tom can watch a user encounter a problem in real time, simultaneously query the live code base, and determine immediately whether it is a bug or a comprehension gap — a "feedback loop on steroids."

- **AI enables the IC CPO model to scale.** The historical objection — "leadership can't stay hands-on at scale, you need more layers" — is less true with AI. You can query ground truth rapidly, which keeps senior people genuinely informed without requiring armies of middle managers to relay information.

- **Top-down leadership works when leadership has genuine ground-truth knowledge.** The failure mode ("micromanagement") comes from leaders managing from above without accurate ground-truth data. When a leader actually knows what's happening in the code, the data, and the customer experience, top-down is efficient. Example: Whatnot CEO Grant regularly clears his schedule mid-review to sit directly with the team, pull the data line by line, and make a decision on the spot.

- **Verify then trust, not trust but verify.** Whatnot operates in a "verify then trust" culture — leaders earn the ability to delegate by deeply understanding systems first. Tom positions himself as T-shaped: very broad across buyer/seller/trust systems, but able to go very deep on any of them when required.

- **The two-dads problem.** When a founder CEO is actively engaged on a project, Tom deliberately steps back and does not add a second layer of review. Having two senior reviewers sending conflicting signals is a known failure mode.

- **Product theater is real and widespread.** Many people with the PM title have spent the last 5 years getting good at communicating frameworks to leadership rather than building actual product skills. The case study in Whatnot's interview process (give a data prompt, ask for a PRD, then verbally defend it) quickly separates those who are good at the theater from those with real product depth.

- **Hire great people and get out of their way is wrong as stated.** The correct version: understand the system deeply enough to know when to trust your team and when to push. Pure delegation without ground-truth engagement leads to bad macro decisions.

- **The core PM skills are becoming more valuable, not less,** even as AI takes over execution. Identifying what to build, distilling requirements, prioritizing for ROI, understanding business strategy — these are deeply human and judgment-driven. The problem is that many people with PM titles have not actually developed these skills.

- **Twitter lessons:**
  1. True product-market fit is an emotional feeling — fervor from users — not "the graphs look okay." Twitter had it so strongly that massive organizational dysfunction couldn't kill it.
  2. "Most of the time you hear it's really complex, it isn't — leadership's just weak." The 140-character limit debate ran for years with everyone knowing the answer but no one willing to make the call.

- **Averages are a common failure mode.** A feature used by only 3% of users looks like a candidate for deprecation — until you discover that 3% are power users for whom it's 100% of their workflow. Deprecating it destroys their use case, with ripple effects across network effects. "Averages lie to you all the time."

- **The future product team at Whatnot** likely looks similar structurally (specialist engineers, designers, PMs) for core high-conviction projects, but with far more "free space" at the edges where anyone — designer, engineer, data scientist — who understands the customer and code base is empowered to ship improvements without PM involvement.

- **On agentic commerce vs. live commerce:** Tom believes agentic AI will dominate high-intent, programmatic purchasing (light bulbs, cables, air filters). But ~80% of US retail spend is still in-person because most shopping is low-intent and social. Live commerce captures the scale/convenience of the internet combined with the social/cultural experience of physical retail — a distinct and durable market.

## Patterns & frameworks

**1. "We regret that product management exists" (Hiring philosophy)**
A founding principle that treats PM as a regrettable necessity rather than a default org building block. The purpose is to force the question "do we actually need a PM here?" before every hire, rather than filling a ratio. It comes with a matching cultural expectation that engineers, designers, and others go through product review and own DRI accountability for features without requiring a PM to be present.

**2. The Pod Ratio Problem**
The observation that tech companies adopted a formula (1 PM + 1 designer + 1 EM per ~6 engineers) not because it was optimal but because scale demanded a delegation mechanism. At billion-DAU products, this ratio produces massive PM headcount, much of it unnecessary — particularly for infrastructure teams where engineering judgment is sufficient.

**3. Product Muscle / Reps Model**
Product judgment is a muscle built through reps. PMs get better by making decisions repeatedly. But abstracting engineers and designers from those decisions causes their muscles to atrophy. The implication: fewer PMs = stronger product muscles across the whole team.

**4. The Accordion (Zoom-out / Zoom-in Framework)**
Before shipping, stretch the accordion fully out: what are we trying to accomplish, what are the knock-on effects, what is the macro implication? Then push it all the way in to V1 — the smallest thing that validates the hypothesis. After shipping and learning, pull it back out again. Repeat. The value is created only on the compression (shipping), but you cannot play the right music without the expansion (thinking). Prevents two failure modes: iterating blindly without direction, and writing long strategic roadmaps that ignore learning.

**5. Know Then Go**
A decision-making principle: think through everything that could go wrong at scale before building — legal, finance, cross-team impacts, failure modes. You do not have to solve all of them; you just have to think through them. Then move. This preempts blockers that would otherwise surface in review and slow teams down, while making the "go" decision faster and more confident.

**6. Macro + Micro Thinking Test (Hiring filter)**
In interviews, Whatnot looks for candidates who can demonstrate both system-level thinking ("here is how the system works and the end state I'm targeting") AND granular impatience ("here is exactly how I would validate that tomorrow"). Candidates who can only do one — either visionaries who never ship or executors who can't see the system — are filtered out.

**7. DRI-to-Problems, not DRI-to-Teams**
Rather than assigning PMs permanently to engineering teams (which creates local optimization and political attachment), Whatnot assigns PMs to specific problems or projects for roughly 6-month cycles. After a planning cycle, each priority gets a named DRI who is accountable for delivery. This keeps PMs rotating across problem spaces, building broader muscles, and prevents the "this is my team's roadmap" mindset.

**8. Verify Then Trust (Leadership model)**
Leaders operate in "verify then trust" mode — they actively maintain ground-truth understanding of the systems they oversee through direct engagement (querying the code base, sitting in on tickets, reviewing raw data) before trusting team outputs. Contrasted with "trust but verify" (passive delegation with spot-checks) and pure delegation (which produces uninformed macro decisions).

**9. The Two-Dads Problem (Founder-CPO dynamics)**
When a founder is actively engaged on a project, adding a CPO layer creates conflicting direction signals for the team. Tom's rule: if Grant or Logan (Whatnot's founders) are on something, he steps out completely. No value in an extra review layer when the most context-rich person is already engaged.

**10. Averages Lie / Individual Use Case Test**
Before deprecating or changing any feature, segment the data beyond the average. A 3% adoption rate looks like dead weight until you discover it is 100% of a specific power-user segment's workflow. Always ask: who are the people for whom this is everything, and what happens to them and the network if we remove it?
