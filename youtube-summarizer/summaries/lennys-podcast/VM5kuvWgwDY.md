# Everyone’s shipping more. Does any of it matter? | Claire Vo

Video ID: `VM5kuvWgwDY`

## Summary
Claire Vo, a product leader with over two decades of experience, argues that the traditional product roadmap is now a liability in the age of AI-powered development. Her core thesis is that execution capacity has outpaced the ability to discover truly meaningful things to build — shifting the bottleneck from "what can we build?" to "what is actually worth building?" She introduces the concept of "roadmap zero" (when everything on the backlog becomes plausible and buildable) and proposes replacing feature-list roadmaps with conviction-driven, ambition-first thinking. The talk is most relevant to product managers, product leaders, and executives navigating how to apply AI development tools without losing strategic focus.

## Key insights
- **The bottleneck has flipped.** Engineering capacity used to be the scarce resource; PMs existed largely to prioritize against that constraint. Now, execution is abundant — the scarce resource is conviction about what is genuinely worth building.
- **Shipping more ≠ revenue growth.** Claire directly asked the audience who is shipping more than ever *and* seeing proportionate revenue growth — the implication being almost nobody raised their hand. Volume of output has decoupled from business outcomes.
- **Claire's personal confession:** She has 40 Grok bots, access to top coding agents, customer context via API/CLI/MCP, and can build almost anything — yet describes herself as "straight up out of good ideas." She built a full "product graph" / insights engine (ChatPRD feature) that was nearly feature-complete vs. competitors in a near-one-shot build, then concluded it "belongs in the trash" because it wasn't differentiated.
- **The three AI-era traps:**
  - **The Backlog Trap** — AI will build everything on your backlog. Clearing requests doesn't mean meaningful business progress.
  - **The Parity Trap** — All competitors talk to the same customers, ingest the same insights, and arrive at the same obvious product decisions. AI accelerates convergence toward competitive parity, not differentiation.
  - **The Churn Trap** — Ship something, see noise, abandon it quickly because you can always ship something else. You never learn or compound on what you've shipped.
- **All three traps feel productive from the inside** — backlog is shrinking, competitive features are rising, velocity is up — but they accelerate your "path to mid" (mediocrity).
- **"Roadmap zero"** isn't an empty backlog. It's when everything on the backlog becomes both plausible and buildable simultaneously, making effort-based prioritization meaningless as a proxy for strategy.
- **The remaining constraint is truth.** AI cannot convert an untested assumption into fact. No adversarial review substitutes for real customers and real data. The obligation to encounter reality in the market increases as build speed increases.
- **Bad ideas now ship faster.** An AI factory paired with an old-style roadmap will reach all three traps at machine speed. Weak judgment's consequences arrive at your door faster than ever.
- **"Durable convictions, disposable features."** The key reframe: bet on a vision and direction, but hold zero ego about specific solutions. Features can come and go; the conviction should hold.
- **Good stubborn vs. bad stubborn:** Good stubborn = stay with the problem, revise the solution. Bad stubborn = use cheap tokens to keep moving goalposts ("we're close, just ship again").
- **Not every ship is a promise.** Claire proposes three tiers of commitment: *probes* (exploratory bets), *experiments* (things you'll stick with until you get right), and *promises* (shipped, stable, customers can rely on and build on).
- **Customer trust is the real scarce resource.** Claire kept her product graph feature-flagged off even after building it because she lacked conviction — she didn't want to burn customer trust by shipping something she wasn't confident in.
- **The velocity game is over; the ambition game is next.** The last 12–18 months were about building the muscle for raw shipping velocity. The next phase should be about taking huge swings — running experiments in 2–3 weeks that previously would have taken a year.
- **New OKR framing:** Instead of measuring PRs or revenue-per-headcount, she suggests asking: "How many big swings are you taking per month?" — with the explicit presumption that most won't work out.
- **The factory is necessary but not sufficient.** She's not against AI-powered factories — she loves them — but insists they belong *after* conviction-building in the process, not before.

- **Teams that have burned through their backlog** and are wondering what to do next — this reframes the problem as a discovery and conviction deficit, not a capacity problem.
- **Startup founders** deciding how to allocate AI-assisted engineering capacity against an uncertain, fast-moving market.
- **Product orgs competing in crowded markets** where AI is erasing differentiation by enabling all players to build the same features simultaneously.
- **Enterprise product teams** having to communicate to stakeholders (boards, customers, sales) about roadmap commitments in an environment where features may need to come and go.
- **Anyone conducting roadmap planning cycles** for 2026 and questioning whether the traditional format still serves strategic decision-making.

## Patterns & frameworks

**Roadmap Zero**
The state where every item on the backlog is simultaneously plausible and buildable due to AI execution capacity. At this point, effort-based prioritization loses meaning as a strategic filter. The traditional roadmap's function (sequencing under scarcity) breaks down entirely.

**The Three AI-Era Traps**
A diagnostic framework for identifying when high-velocity shipping is creating an illusion of progress:
1. *Backlog Trap* — building everything on the list without testing whether it matters
2. *Parity Trap* — converging on the same product as every competitor through shared customer insights and AI tooling
3. *Churn Trap* — abandoning features before compounding learning, because shipping something new is always possible

**Conviction-First Roadmap**
A replacement for the feature/date roadmap, structured around:
1. Define durable convictions (where is the world going in 1–2 years?)
2. Pre-define what evidence would confirm or falsify the conviction
3. Build/maintain the factory (execution capacity)
4. Allocate tokens/effort/investment based on conviction strength, not estimated effort

**The Probe / Experiment / Promise Spectrum**
A commitment-signaling framework for features:
- *Probe* — exploratory, low commitment, openly a bet
- *Experiment* — medium commitment, team believes in it and will iterate to get it right
- *Promise* — shipped, stable, customers can rely on and build upon it
Honesty about which tier a feature sits in replaces false precision of roadmap dates.

**Good Stubborn vs. Bad Stubborn**
A decision heuristic for when to persist:
- *Good stubborn* = stay committed to the problem/conviction, revise the solution freely
- *Bad stubborn* = use cheap execution to keep shipping variants without updating your conviction, effectively moving the goalposts

**Durable Convictions + Disposable Features**
A mental model for product strategy: hold strong, long-term directional beliefs while maintaining zero ego about specific solutions. Progress is measured against the conviction, not against whether a particular feature survived.

**Ambition as the New Velocity Metric**
A proposed shift in how teams measure productivity: from "how many PRs/features shipped" to "how many large, ambitious experiments are we running per month" — with the baseline assumption that most will fail, and that's the point.
