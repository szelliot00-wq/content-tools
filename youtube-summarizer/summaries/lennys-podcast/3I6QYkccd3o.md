# Why every company now needs to think and operate like a lab team | Josh Woodward (VP Google Labs)

Video ID: `3I6QYkccd3o`

## Summary
Josh Woodward, VP of Google Labs, argues that every company — not just tech giants — must now operate like a labs team because the pace of AI-driven change makes continuous experimentation a survival requirement. He draws on over four years running Google's longest-standing and largest labs team (which oversees zero-to-one experiments, the Gemini app, and AI Studio) to share specific, battle-tested lessons. The conversation covers idea generation, product-market fit signals, when to kill ideas, team structure, hiring, and the evolving value of different skills in the AI era. It is most relevant to product leaders, startup founders, and anyone tasked with building or running an innovation function inside an organization.

---

## Key insights

- **Every company needs a frontier team.** A dedicated group whose explicit job is to stay at the bleeding edge — using every new tool, following every model release — so the rest of the company can stay focused. These are "the people who have eight $200/month subscriptions on everything."
- **Good ideas don't come from design sprints or calendar blocks.** They emerge from people who are obsessively hacking on side projects, sharing demos in hallways, building over weekends. You can't microwave good ideas.
- **Maintain a "what's almost possible" list.** Josh's team tracks a running doc of capabilities that are nearly — but not yet — achievable. When something crosses the threshold, it acts like a phase shift (solid to gas). They also maintain 82 predictions starting with "We believe the future is..." knowing that 9 out of 10 will be wrong, but that having a point of view is essential.
- **Product-market fit is an art, not a science — look at people's eyes.** When showing early prototypes, the real metric is whether someone's eyes light up, whether they lean in. It is not DAU, MAU, or D7 retention. Pupils dilate. You know it when you see it.
- **Fall in love with the problem, not the product.** Most successful products require 3–5 pivots. Teams that anchor to a specific solution too early almost always have a bad time.
- **The team knows before the leader does when to kill an idea.** Signs include passion running out, exhausted options, and the general feeling that "it's just not working" — like knowing a relationship isn't working. Leaders should create psychological safety for the team to surface this honestly. Josh cited a PM who stopped a near-launch feature because the early data wasn't there, and he publicly praised her for it.
- **Unlearning rate is the skill signal to watch.** More valuable than raw learning speed is how fast someone can learn something and then walk away from it when the evidence changes. This is the key mispriced signal in hiring right now.
- **"Explosive endurance" as a team health concept.** Borrowed from a Roger Federer biography: the ability to go very hard and sustain it over years, not burn out in nine months. Labs uses "seasons" — naming when a team is in sprint mode vs. recovery/exploration mode — to manage this.
- **Labs teams historically fail in one of two ways:** they fizzle after 3–4 years, or they produce ideas that the parent company never commercializes. The hardest part is bridging the lab's frontier work back to the core organization in a way that's taken seriously, not dismissed as "gimmicks and toys."
- **Labs team size has shrunk with AI.** What used to require 5–7 people now requires 2–3. Think of it as super-talented musicians who team up in different combinations to play different songs.
- **PMs are adapting to AI faster than other functions** because PM is inherently horizontal and PMs are already accustomed to simulating other roles (lawyer, BD, sales). But Josh warns against the "everyone is a builder now" narrative — specialists still matter, and losing respect for expertise is a real risk. Frame it as a major/minor: coding and building are the minor, your core function is still your major.
- **Labs should create "good trouble."** Labs should be used not just for product development but to challenge how the company itself operates — new job ladders, new ways of working — and pilot those internally before rolling them out company-wide.
- **Planning horizon is rolling six months, with 50–100 day sprints.** Models release roughly every six months, and the best labs teams go from idea to meaningful milestone in 50–100 days. Multi-year vision exists, but the two-year roadmap is largely meaningless given how fast things change.
- **Zero-to-one, one-to-ten, and ten-to-hundred are distinct stages** with different challenges, different people, and different metrics. A key risk as labs projects scale is that larger products crowd out the zero-to-one work unless you explicitly protect it with distinct stage labels and resources.
- **Model benchmarks are overhyped.** Most users will never care about ELO ratings. The industry over-rotates to speeds and feeds at the expense of asking "is this actually useful to someone?"
- **Principles and values in product development are underhyped.** Josh wrote an internal doc on why Labs builds what it builds. Products encode principles — and there isn't enough industry conversation about what kind of future we actually want to build.
- **Team awards encode culture.** Josh gives physical awards to reinforce values: a miniature rake (TPU harvester) for efficiency work, a golden band-aid for fixing paper cuts, and a giant set of ears + mechanical keyboard for "LLM whisperers" who coax breakthrough behaviors from models. There's also a secret-society-style "appreciation meeting" where leaders ambush a team member with a circle of gratitude — no agenda listed on the invite.

## Use cases

- **Startup founders** deciding where to focus R&D energy and how to structure experimentation without burning out the team.
- **Product leaders at mid-size or large companies** who need to build or justify an internal innovation/labs function to leadership.
- **PMs early in their careers** being encouraged to vibe-code and prototype — use these as tools to become a better PM, not as replacements for PM craft.
- **Engineering and design leaders** thinking about how roles are blurring and how to structure teams of 2–4 "builder" generalists without losing functional depth.
- **Executives and CEOs** deciding how to structure a labs team — specifically where it should report, how independent it needs to be, and what its mandate is (graduation lab vs. new product lines).
- **Anyone hiring for innovation roles**, looking for better signals than resumes — specifically unlearning rate, obsessive building, and how someone navigates unstructured ambiguity.
- **Team leads managing burnout** in fast-moving environments who need a framework for naming sprint vs. recovery seasons.
- **Consumer product builders** looking for where the next major opportunity might be: entertainment, messaging, personal AI agents, or experiences that bring people together IRL.

## Patterns & frameworks

**"What's Almost Possible" list**
A running document tracking capabilities that exist just below the threshold of practical usefulness. When something crosses over, the team treats it as a phase transition — a signal to immediately mobilize around it. The complementary tool is 82 forward-looking predictions ("We believe the future is..."), accepting a ~90%+ failure rate as the cost of having a useful directional view.

**Zero-to-One / One-to-Ten / Ten-to-Hundred staging**
Labs explicitly labels projects by lifecycle stage. Zero-to-one uses eye-contact and gut metrics. One-to-ten begins adding conversion and retention thinking. Ten-to-hundred is a scaling game with CPAs and funnel optimization. Each stage attracts and requires different types of people, and the framework protects early-stage work from being crowded out by scaled products.

**Explosive Endurance**
A mindset/culture model for sustaining high performance over years rather than months. Borrowed from the Roger Federer biography. Operationalized through "seasons" — explicitly naming when a team is in full-sprint mode vs. in a recovery/exploration phase (e.g., post-Google IO = go hack, no shipping pressure). The key move is naming the mode publicly so people can opt in or out consciously.

**Users First / Google Second / Labs Third**
A prioritization hierarchy that prevents labs from becoming self-serving. Success is defined as making users and the broader company successful — not the lab itself. This keeps the lab collaborative with the core business instead of isolated and precious.

**The Eye Test for PMF**
In early prototype stages, the primary metric is whether a person's eyes light up, whether they lean in, whether their pupils dilate. This replaces quantitative metrics (which don't exist yet) with a direct physical signal of genuine engagement vs. polite tolerance.

**Unlearning Rate as a hiring signal**
Rather than asking "how fast do you learn?", ask "how fast can you learn something and then discard it when the evidence changes?" This surfaces intellectual flexibility and avoidance of sunk-cost thinking — especially valuable in environments where the underlying technology changes every few months.

**Good Trouble vs. Bad Trouble**
Labs should deliberately challenge existing norms and processes inside the company — but in service of improvement, not disruption for its own sake. Example: Labs was the first team at Google to pilot a "builder job ladder" that acknowledged blurring roles, with the intent of eventually rolling it out company-wide.

**Major/Minor framing for role evolution**
As AI makes building more accessible across functions, people should think of their core function (PM, design, engineering) as their "major" and new builder/coding skills as their "minor." This prevents the trap of losing functional expertise in pursuit of being a generalist builder.
