# Elena Verna: The case for becoming a HI-IC | Elena Verna (Lovable)

Video ID: `fn8wnmpVqeI`

## Summary
Elena Verna, Head of Growth at Lovable, makes the case for senior leaders voluntarily (or involuntarily) stepping back from people management into a new role she calls the High-Impact Individual Contributor (HI-IC). Drawing on her own experience being "fired" out of a management role at Lovable, she argues that AI has fundamentally broken the old equation of "more reports = more impact," replacing it with "more you can ship = more impact." The talk outlines why this transition is emotionally difficult, what organizational conditions must exist for it to work, and why she believes it represents the most exciting career opportunity of the current era. It is most relevant to senior ICs, VPs, directors, and heads of growth/product/marketing who are considering whether the management ladder is truly the right path for them.

## Key insights
- **The old career ladder equation is broken.** For decades, career advancement meant managing more people — IC → manager → director → VP, with headcount as the only currency of impact. AI is dissolving this constraint.
- **The new equation:** How much you can ship = how much impact you can drive. One person with AI can now execute what previously required a full cross-functional team (PM, designer, engineer, analyst).
- **Elena's definition of HI-IC:** Not just a senior person with no direct reports, but someone who can find a problem, make a decision, execute *across all functions required*, ship it, own the outcome, and iterate — essentially a whole team's charter in one person.
- **AI = "Average Intelligence" at your fingertips.** She frames AI not as AGI but as having an average marketer, engineer, analyst, and designer available on demand. She notes that's sometimes better than some teams she has managed — sufficient to get to learning/feedback, which is the real goal.
- **Only 1–2 areas of exceptional craft are needed now;** "good enough" with AI handles the rest — because the purpose of shipping is to learn from customer feedback, not to achieve perfection.
- **The old build cycle:** Idea → meeting → PM → designer → meeting → engineer → review → approval → ship (~3 months minimum). The new cycle: Idea → build → learn. Elena does all of this inside Lovable itself.
- **"When the cost of building falls below the cost of coordinating, the org chart should change."** She challenges leaders: adopting AI tools without changing organizational design means leaving the biggest advantage on the table.
- **Elena's personal journey:** She was fired out of her management role at Lovable (not out of the company). Her job has changed three times in ~15 months. Lovable reorgs every few weeks. She frames this instability as a feature of living through an era comparable to the Industrial Revolution.
- **Identity crisis is real.** She spent ~2 weeks wondering whether she had been promoted or had lost status. Two decades of conditioning tied her identity to her title and team size. She acknowledges this openly and encourages others not to let title define self-worth, especially as all jobs are changing.
- **Her current day-to-day as HI-IC:** She personally owns pricing and packaging, builds and deploys the pricing page to production, creates prototypes, does user research, runs analysis, and handles optimizations — collaborating with engineers only for deeper model changes.
- **90% of her time is building or thinking about what to build next** — versus the meeting/dependency hell of her management days.
- **42 out of 51 people managers she surveyed wanted to go back to an IC role.** Many became managers not because they wanted to, but because it was the only path to advancement.
- **The cost of terrible managers:** Many people became managers because the ladder forced it, not because they were suited for it. Removing that forced path could eliminate a lot of bad management.
- **She believes ICs should be paid *more* than managers** in an AI-native world, because ICs can now drive more outcome than managers in the old system. Pay and status must be decoupled from headcount to make the HI-IC path viable.
- **Don't try to be both manager and IC simultaneously.** She has tried it; the context switch is nearly impossible. You must pick a path.
- **Learning the IC role makes you a better future manager.** She notes she couldn't effectively manage people doing IC work today if she didn't understand what that work now looks like.
- **AI's most interesting organizational effect is not job replacement — it's separating impact from headcount.**

## Use cases
- A VP or Director who got promoted because they were excellent at their craft but now spends 90% of their time in meetings and feels drained — this framework offers a legitimate path back to hands-on work without a pay cut or loss of status.
- A founder or CEO deciding whether to hire a VP-level leader as a traditional people manager or as a HI-IC who can ship independently.
- A growth, product, or marketing leader evaluating whether to rebuild a team or instead operate as a high-leverage solo function with AI tooling.
- An organization considering restructuring away from heavy management layers toward flatter, more autonomous teams.
- A senior IC already doing multi-functional work with AI who wants language and a framework to advocate for leadership-level compensation without a management title.
- A people manager who feels trapped in a role they didn't truly want, looking for validation and a practical path back to craft work.
- Any org designing compensation structures in an AI-native environment where headcount no longer maps cleanly to impact.

## Patterns & frameworks

**High-Impact Individual Contributor (HI-IC)**
Elena's central model. A HI-IC is not a standard senior IC with no direct reports — it is someone who independently identifies problems, makes decisions, executes across all necessary functions (design, engineering, analysis, marketing), ships, and owns outcomes end-to-end. This is what previously required a 5–10 person cross-functional team.

**AI as "Average Intelligence"**
A mental model for how to think about AI's practical value today: not AGI, but an on-demand average practitioner in every discipline. The insight is that "good enough across functions" is sufficient because the goal of shipping is learning, not perfection. This reframes AI from a threat into a force multiplier for the single high-craft individual.

**Cost of Building vs. Cost of Coordinating**
A decision heuristic for org design: when the cost of building something falls below the cost of coordinating people to build it, the org chart should change. This is the structural trigger that makes the HI-IC model rational at an organizational level.

**The Five Conditions for HI-IC to Work (Organizational Operating System)**
Elena's checklist for whether a company is actually set up to support HI-IC roles:
1. **Radical information access** — No management layers gatekeeping information; everyone has what they need to make decisions.
2. **Authority travels with accountability** — ICs can decide, ship, and fail without multi-level approval chains. Meaningful failures (with learning) are tolerated.
3. **Scope defined by outcome, not function** — HI-ICs aren't siloed; they go wherever the problem is (growth, marketing, core product, etc.).
4. **Approval chains are the expensive part** — If experiments are cheap to run, make approval the exception, not the rule.
5. **Pay and status decoupled from headcount** — ICs must be compensated at leadership-level salaries; otherwise rational self-interest will push everyone into management.

**Management as Career Path, Not Promotion**
A reframe of the management track: management should be a distinct career *choice* with its own criteria and appeal — not the default next rung on a ladder that forces craftspeople out of the work they're good at. The closing line of the talk: "Management needs to be a career path, not a promotion."
