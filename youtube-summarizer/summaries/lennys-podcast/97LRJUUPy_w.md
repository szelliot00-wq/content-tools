# 90 minutes of unfiltered product advice from Snap and Discord’s product chief | Peter Sellis

Video ID: `97LRJUUPy_w`

## Summary
This is a wide-ranging conversation between Lenny Rachitsky and Peter Sellis — former first PM at Snapchat (7 years) and head of product at Discord — recorded while Sellis was between roles and free from corporate PR constraints. Sellis shares unfiltered lessons on team design, product management quality, consumer monetization, growth strategy, and what makes great PMs. The conversation is most relevant to product leaders, founders, and anyone building or scaling consumer products.

---

## Key insights

- **Design teams like a terrorist organization:** Two key traits make this effective — (1) an ideological, almost religious clarity of mission so every person understands *why* they're doing what they're doing, and (2) a crystal-clear org structure defining who can be trusted with which decisions. This minimizes collaboration overhead and lets teams move fast without constant escalation.

- **Collaboration has a hidden cost nobody internalizes:** No executive ever says "stop collaborating," but coordination costs ensure you move as slowly as the slowest node. Sellis aggressively accounts for this cost when designing orgs.

- **Most PMs are bad — mathematically:** Product management skill is normally distributed across the population, but only the right-tail people become PMs — making it a power distribution where the median is well below the mean. Compounding this: the best PMs exit the profession (become founders/execs), while mediocre ones have every incentive to stay given high ZIRP-era comp. The profession is structurally set up for a low median.

- **Snap's monetization failure had three specific causes:**
  1. Audience skews very young (under 18), and there's a sharp CPM discontinuity at age 17→18→21 due to tracking restrictions for minors.
  2. Snap opens to the camera (creation-first), not a feed (consumption-first) — there's no natural ad placement in the core flow.
  3. At its core, Snap is a messaging utility, and messaging apps are historically terrible at monetization.

- **Snap is actually an extraordinary product portfolio:** #1 messaging app for US teens, #1 camera app, used more than Apple/Google Maps by under-21s, top astrology app. The chip-on-shoulder argument: Snap scaled its ads business faster than Twitter, Pinterest, and Reddit despite starting later and operating with a harder format and audience.

- **Snapchat Plus launched too late:** Subscription products for messaging utilities compound slowly — users need to be deeply habituated before they see value in paying. Launching Snap+ 4 years earlier could have reduced pressure on ads, freeing product teams to compete better with TikTok.

- **Growth comes from the core, not expansion:** At both Snap and Discord, the fastest growth came from doubling down on existing core users — making the product faster and more performant for people already using it daily. At Discord, focusing on "intentional multiplayer gamers" in 2024 drove some of the fastest post-pandemic growth, even though everyone assumed that cohort was already 100% penetrated.

- **DAU/MAU is the core growth lever for consumer products:** Moving someone from using the product 10 out of 30 days to 11, 12, or 13 days is "monster growth." Acquiring new monthly actives is far less efficient than deepening retention of existing daily users.

- **Ride your best people into the ground:** Counter to human nature (which wants to save the drowning), Sellis loads his strongest performers with more and more decisions until they start making bad ones. He has little mechanism for improving weak performers — he essentially lets them fail on their own.

- **Saying no is a muscle of taste:** Evan Spiegel personally said no to better ideas than most consumer startups ever launch. A great museum curator keeps most of the collection off the walls. Taste is best measured by asking someone what they *built but never shipped* — not what failed in testing, but what they held and consciously chose not to release.

- **AI is bad at removal:** AI adds, it doesn't subtract. As AI makes building easier, the PM skill of knowing what *not* to ship becomes more rare and more valuable.

- **Last-mover advantage in ads:** OpenAI is "speedrunning" ads infrastructure that Meta spent a decade building. The key innovation from Meta was making organic value/relevance a factor in auction bid pricing, not just the highest dollar bid. In a chat context, this translates to *trust* — how much trust the system trades away to serve an ad.

- **SpaceX as the anti-innovator's-dilemma company:** SpaceX is actively sunsetting the Falcon 9 — the greatest launch system ever built — to force adoption of Starship. This is Cortez-level "burn the ships." The culture top-to-bottom embraces it. The 10-year product roadmap has always been public — a mark of extreme ambition.

- **Long-term greedy (ads principle):** Short-term revenue in ads is tempting (lower prices, show more ads) but advertiser ROI today is causally linked to revenue tomorrow. "Long-term greedy" was a phrase used to give teams cover to make short-term sacrifices for compounding long-term growth.

- **Core Product Value (CPV) framework:** Define in one sentence what makes someone come back to your product every day, then break it into measurable metrics. Snap: *"The fastest way to share a moment with the people you care about."* Discord: *"The best way to talk and hang out with your friends before, during, and after playing games."* The phrase must map directly to metrics that data scientists can track.

- **The three PM oxymorons (great PMs hold contradictory traits simultaneously):**
  1. Confident bordering on arrogant + genuinely humble, generous with credit
  2. Highly organized and process-driven + totally comfortable with ambiguity, almost pathologically anti-authority
  3. Long-term thinker who knows what building blocks need to come into place + borderline-annoying bias for action day-to-day

- **Systems thinking = model it in a spreadsheet:** Rather than "asking why 5 times," real systems thinking means mapping stocks, flows, variables, levers, and probability distributions in a quantitative model. Jay Forrester's system dynamics is more useful than pop-science slinky books.

- **Backcasting over forecasting for innovation:** Instead of incrementally extrapolating from today, envision the ideal future state unconstrained by current technology, then work backwards. This is how Snap got to glasses/AR. Very few people can do this without defaulting to remixing the present.

## Patterns & frameworks

**1. Terrorist Organization Team Design**
Two components: (1) ideological clarity — everyone knows *why* at a religious level; (2) clear org structure — who owns which decisions. Minimizes coordination cost, enables autonomous fast execution without over-collaboration.

**2. Core Product Value (CPV)**
A single sentence defining why users return daily, structured so it breaks into trackable metrics. Format: [adjective/verb] + [action] + [relationship/context]. Example: "Fastest way to share a moment with the people you care about." Metrics definitions are mapped to the phrase components. Used in onboarding, roadmap prioritization, and growth books.

**3. Long-Term Greedy (Ads)**
A decision-making heuristic for ads teams: prioritize advertiser ROI today even at the cost of short-term revenue, because advertiser ROI is causally upstream of long-term revenue growth. Gives teams ideological cover to resist short-term revenue pressure.

**4. Ride Your Winners (Talent Allocation)**
Counterintuitively, invest management time and decision-making weight in your strongest performers by loading them with more responsibility until they break — rather than trying to improve struggling performers. Based on the idea that confidence is self-reinforcing and strong people have more runway to absorb challenge.

**5. DAU/MAU as Growth Lever**
Frame growth not as new user acquisition but as moving existing users up the frequency ladder (e.g., 10-day → 13-day monthly actives). Even small changes in DAU/MAU ratios compound dramatically because daily users are worth 30x monthly users by pure usage math.

**6. Museum Curator Model of Product Taste**
The value of great taste is *what you don't ship*, not what you do. A full museum (all features launched) means the curator did nothing. Test for taste in interviews by asking what someone built, loved, and chose not to release.

**7. Backcasting for Innovation**
Instead of forecasting forward from the present, define the ideal future state unconstrained by current technology, then reason backwards to identify what needs to exist. Contrasted with incremental "remixing the present." Most useful for identifying platform-level bets (e.g., AR glasses vs. phone camera).

**8. The Three PM Oxymorons**
A framework for identifying great PMs by the three pairs of contradictory traits they hold in tension: (1) confidence + humility, (2) process-orientation + comfort with ambiguity, (3) long-term vision + short-term bias for action.

**9. Concentric Circle Expansion**
When deciding whether to expand beyond the core product, start with: (a) what core capabilities could serve adjacent cohorts, and (b) where are users already hacking the product to do something you didn't design for? Those are the natural expansion vectors before attempting blue-sky innovation.

**10. Systems Thinking via Spreadsheet Modeling**
Operationalize systems thinking by building a quantitative model: define all variables, stocks, flows, levers, and probability distributions. A spreadsheet model — even one that never gets used — forces clarity about causality and distributions that verbal "asking why 5 times" cannot achieve.
