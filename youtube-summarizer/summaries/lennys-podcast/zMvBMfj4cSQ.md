# AI’s third era: the rise of persistent AI coworkers | Tara Seshan (OpenAI’s product lead)

Video ID: `zMvBMfj4cSQ`

## Summary
This episode of Lenny's Podcast features Tara Seshan, OpenAI's product lead for Codex and ChatGPT Work, discussing how AI is reshaping product management, knowledge work, and organizational culture. She frames AI product evolution in three eras — chat, agents, and persistent AI coworkers — and argues that the core PM skill of sharp hypothesis formation and rapid empirical testing has never mattered more. The conversation covers how to build for a fast-moving model landscape, why ambition is now the primary differentiator, and how ChatGPT's Work mode aims to bring agent-level capabilities to a billion users. Most relevant to PMs, founders, engineers, and knowledge workers navigating the AI transition.

## Key insights
- **Three eras of AI products:** Era 1 = chat, Era 2 = agents (primarily coding), Era 3 (emerging) = persistent AI coworkers that collaborate with you and your teammates simultaneously, functioning more like colleagues than tools.
- **Build for 2–3 months out, not now and not a year from now.** Building for current model capabilities makes your product obsolete immediately; building for a year out means you're speculating too far. The two-to-three-month window is the only viable planning horizon.
- **The PM core never changed — only the speed did.** The essential job remains: form a sharp hypothesis, design the fastest possible test, observe results, refine, repeat. What changed is that all the surrounding "trappings" (long strategy docs, polished presentations) have been stripped away, leaving only this loop.
- **Empirical over academic.** In slow-moving markets (e.g., payments), rigorous long-form reasoning docs are a competitive advantage. In AI, they are a liability. Speed of testing beats depth of planning. Tara describes the transition from "PhD thesis docs" to "how fast can I get something in front of users" as jarring but necessary.
- **Steering vs. rowing.** As agents handle more tactical work (rowing), the human role increasingly becomes steering — setting direction, making opinionated calls, and providing feedback at higher and higher levels of abstraction. Tara expects the steering layer to keep moving upward.
- **Ambition as the new differentiator.** When everyone has access to the same tools, what separates individuals and companies is how ambitious they choose to be. The capabilities ceiling has risen dramatically; the bottleneck is now expanding your thinking to match it.
- **Elevating others' ambitions is now a core PM responsibility.** Tara identifies reminding teammates of what's possible — pushing timelines faster, scoping bigger — as one of the most important things a PM can do today.
- **OpenAI's three internal memes of product development:** (1) "Is this maximally accelerated?" — are we moving as fast as possible? (2) "Are you mainlining it?" — are you using the product all day, every day, dogfooding constantly? (3) Elevating ambitions — are we being as ambitious as the ceiling allows?
- **Founder mentality at scale.** OpenAI operates with minimal top-down direction. Every person is effectively a founder of their area; the distance between any employee and the market is unusually thin. There is no "secret strategy bible" — everything becomes public almost immediately.
- **ChatGPT's north star: zero user decisions.** The current Chat/Work/Codex toggle structure is a transitional state. The goal is a single box where users type any task and the system selects the right model and harness automatically.
- **Work mode = Codex for knowledge workers.** The underlying engine of ChatGPT Work is Codex, but with a UI adapted for non-developers — no visible work trees, more focus on citations, reasoning chains, and in-progress visibility, because knowledge work can't be validated by tests alone.
- **Knowledge work ≠ coding in one critical way.** Coding output can be validated via tests. Knowledge work output (a deck, a financial model, a strategy doc) cannot be spot-checked at the end — you need to observe the process, inputs, reasoning, and citations along the way. This fundamentally changes how the product must be designed.
- **Cloud infrastructure is as important as model intelligence.** Persistent cloud agents require solving data access (connecting to Slack, Google Docs, company databases), reliability, and security — unglamorous but equally important to agent effectiveness as raw model capability.
- **The "cozy software" and personal software movement.** Tara is excited about friends building small apps for each other (e.g., a private social network called GATS, a tool that turns articles into podcast episodes). She sees this as the realization of Alan Kay's "personal computer as personal software" vision.
- **Sites as a new daily artifact.** Tara uses ChatGPT/Codex's Sites feature constantly — building interactive tools, games, trip trackers, dashboards, and presentations — replacing docs and slides with dynamic, shareable, database-backed web apps created via a single prompt.
- **Slash /visualize as an underused feature.** Typing `/visualize` in Codex lets you generate rich data visualizations from your ChatGPT usage history or any data source instantly.
- **Writing as thinking vs. writing as reporting.** Tara draws a sharp distinction: writing-as-thinking (briefs, strategy docs, new product ideas) she never automates — the act of outlining, drafting, and editing is the thinking itself. Writing-as-reporting (status updates, launch plans, summaries) she fully delegates to AI.
- **Mocks not docs, prototypes not docs.** At OpenAI, a long document is no longer a credible signal that you've thought something through — because AI can generate one instantly. A prototype, an A/B result, or something people can interact with is now the more trusted artifact.
- **The 70% doc rule.** Take a doc to 70% completion, then bring in stakeholders. A perfectly polished idea repels input; a rough one invites collaboration and co-ownership, producing better outcomes.
- **Product marketing fit precedes product-market fit.** From her time at Sutter Hill Ventures, Tara learned to get the narrative and positioning right first — pitch 100 people, refine the story — before committing to a product shape. She had previously underrated PMM work.
- **AI brain rot defense.** The primary defense against over-reliance on AI is the writing-as-thinking discipline: always write first drafts yourself, always read your own doc before asking anyone else to. The model can help with research, data, or pushback in the middle — but start and end as a human.
- **Role boundaries are dissolving.** Tara embraces this: someone needs to be the DRI (directly responsible individual) for whether the product works and is wanted — regardless of their title. Everything else is fluid. She is unsure whether this dissolves craft or just redirects it.
- **Mobile + cloud agents = magic moment.** Starting a long-running agent task on your phone, losing connectivity on a subway, and emerging to find the work complete is described as the most viscerally magical current product experience.

## Patterns & frameworks

**Three eras of AI products**
Era 1: Chat (static Q&A interface). Era 2: Agents (primarily coding agents doing autonomous tasks). Era 3 (emerging): Persistent AI coworkers — agents that feel like teammates, operate in the cloud continuously, collaborate with multiple humans simultaneously, and get work done between human check-ins.

**Steering vs. rowing**
Agents handle the tactical, repetitive "rowing." Humans handle "steering" — setting direction, making opinionated calls, interpreting results, and deciding what comes next. The steering layer progressively moves to higher levels of abstraction as agents become more capable. Human value concentrates in judgment, taste, and accountability rather than execution.

**The 2–3 month build window**
Building for current model capabilities is wrong (the model will improve). Building for capabilities 12+ months out is also wrong (you're speculating). The only viable target is 2–3 months ahead, informed by close collaboration with research teams on their near-term roadmap.

**The hypothesis-test loop (core PM job)**
(1) Understand users, market, and technology. (2) Form the sharpest possible hypothesis — identify the single most important question ("the eigen question," per Shishir Mehrotra). (3) Design the fastest, most efficient test. (4) Read results. (5) Refine hypothesis and repeat. This loop has not changed; everything else around it has been stripped away.

**Writing-as-thinking vs. writing-as-reporting**
A two-category framework for when to use AI in writing. Writing-as-reporting (status updates, launch plans, summaries, format conversions) = fully automate. Writing-as-thinking (briefs, strategy, new product ideas, spicy takes) = never automate; the act of writing is inseparable from the act of thinking. Rule of thumb: start yourself, end yourself; AI may assist in the middle for research and pushback only.

**The 70% doc rule**
Draft any document to ~70% completion before sharing with stakeholders. A fully polished idea repels input (new ideas "bounce off" it). A rough draft with "crags and rough edges" invites co-authorship, buy-in, and better outcomes. Stakeholders help take it from 70% to 100%.

**Product marketing fit before product-market fit (Sutter Hill model)**
Derived from Mike Speiser's incubation playbook. Before committing to a product shape, get the narrative and positioning right — pitch 100 potential customers, refine the story, and validate that people respond to the framing of the problem and solution. Only then finalize the product. PMM work is not glue between functions; at its best, it is the primary lever for company success.

**OpenAI's three product memes**
Repeatable internal culture shortcuts that encode the operating philosophy: (1) "Is this maximally accelerated?" — challenge every timeline to move faster. (2) "Are you mainlining it?" — are you using your own product every day, constantly? (3) Elevating ambitions — is the scope of what we're attempting as large as the current capability ceiling allows?

**Mocks not docs / prototypes not docs**
In an era where long documents can be generated instantly, they are no longer credible proof of thought. The trusted artifacts are now: something people can interact with, A/B test results, working prototypes. The default artifact for communication has shifted from text to interactive demos and data.

**The cozy/personal software movement**
A cultural pattern, not a product framework: individuals building small, bespoke software tools for themselves and small groups of friends, enabled by AI. The "dream of malleable personal software" (Alan Kay, 1960s) becomes practically accessible via a single prompt. Tara sees this as a meaningful shift in who creates software and why.
