# Move fast without breaking people: Vonny Laing

Video ID: `xnZ4h4wqY9A`

## Summary
Vonny Laing, a service design leader with 20 years of experience across UK public and private sectors, argues that the traditional "optimization and growth playbook" is no longer fit for purpose as the line between consumer products and essential services blurs. Her central thesis is that when a product becomes a lifeline — often without its designers realizing it — the team inherits a duty of care to users whether they choose it or not. She presents two practical, low-cost frameworks — "Eat Your Greens" and "Leave the Building" — to help designers move fast without causing harm. The talk is most relevant to product managers, UX designers, and service designers working on products that touch financial, health, or daily-life decisions for a broad population.

## Key insights
- The line between a consumer product people choose and a service people depend on is blurring — most designers are already building things with higher stakes than they realize.
- The concept of an "average user" or "typical customer" is largely fictional; designing around it means designing for no one in particular and leaving vulnerable groups behind by default.
- 1 in 5 people in the UK live in poverty (13.4 million people, 4 million of them children), defined as having less than 60% of median household income after housing costs — this is the real context many products land in.
- A composite research participant ("envelope mom") — a single working mother studying and raising two kids on very little — demonstrated extraordinary financial sophistication: she tracked every penny across physical envelopes, knew which supermarkets reduced food at which times, and strategically ate the salad at Pizza Hut so she didn't need to buy her own meal. The assumption that people in poverty are bad with money is wrong.
- "Edge cases" is a harmful framing: the people hardest to serve are not statistical outliers — they represent up to 1 in 5 users and their needs are real and complex.
- Designing for the hardest-to-serve users first produces better experiences for everyone: clarity in a banking app helps both a struggling single parent and a millionaire. Plain English helps non-native speakers, anxious users, and people in a hurry equally.
- Dashboards show *what* happens but not *why* — two users clicking in and out of a banking app five times a day look identical in the data but have completely different emotional contexts (discretionary spending vs. anxious penny-counting).
- User *wants* and user *needs* are different things and are frequently conflated: a product might give users the chocolate (the want) while failing to provide the vegetables (the long-term need).
- Most teams never ask what happens to users 6 months or a year after they complete a flow or offboard — barely anyone in the room raised their hand when asked.
- Buy Now Pay Later was cited as a case where frictionlessness was weaponized: making commitment so easy that users couldn't think through consequences, leading to harm. Citizens Advice Bureau data was referenced to illustrate downstream debt consequences.
- Auto-enrollment in pensions is a positive counterexample of designing for the future self — since 25-year-olds can't meaningfully conceive of being old and poor, the system removes the choice burden.
- Speed bumps outside schools are a physical-world analogy for adding intentional friction to protect people from harm caused by optimizing purely for speed.
- Research doesn't have to be expensive: "two people, a train fare, a box of Celebrations chocolates, and a day out of meetings" can return 200 post-it notes of actionable insight without a research platform, repository, or incentive scheme.
- The people most affected by poor service design are often invisible — the envelope mom's kids were "warm, fed, and well-clothed," so no one could see how much she was struggling.
- Products don't exist in isolation — they land alongside rent, council tax, school dinners, bus passes, and groceries, all competing for the same scarce cognitive and financial resources.

## Use cases
- **Product teams in fintech or banking** designing apps, credit products, overdraft features, or payment flows for a broad demographic including low-income users.
- **Public sector digital teams** modernizing legacy government services (benefits, visas, pensions, student loans) where users have no alternative.
- **PMs facing the "no research budget" argument** — the talk provides a low-cost, concrete counter-case for why skipping research is more expensive than doing it.
- **Design leads onboarding teams to inclusive or accessible design**, particularly when moving away from happy-path thinking.
- **Teams building financial products with deferred consequences** — loans, BNPL, mortgages, pensions — where the harm of a bad decision lands months or years after the user interaction.
- **Anyone refreshing or questioning their persona/demographic data**, particularly if those artifacts haven't been updated in a while.
- **Leaders being asked to prioritize features for "core" users** over harder-to-serve segments — this framework gives language and evidence to push back.
- **Teams considering remote-only research** who need justification for in-person, contextual inquiry.

## Patterns & frameworks

**Designing from the Edges (Inclusive Design Inversion)**
Rather than designing for the "average" user and treating harder-to-serve users as afterthoughts, flip the process: start with the people who have the most difficulty, the highest cognitive load, and the least margin for error. Benefits automatically flow upward — what works for the most constrained user works better for everyone. Analogous to how curb cuts, captioning, and plain English improve experiences for all users, not just those with specific needs.

**Eat Your Greens (Needs vs. Wants + Future Self Design)**
User wants get people into a product; user needs (especially future needs) are what keep them safe and served over time. The framework has three components: (1) distinguish wants from needs explicitly, (2) use a three-horizons model — past self, present self, future self — to design across a user's life arc, and (3) follow your design decisions forward by asking "what happens if the user gets exactly what they asked for and nothing else?" at six months, one year, and five years out. Friction can be a feature (speed bumps, auto-enrollment) when it protects the future self.

**Leave the Building (Contextual Inquiry as a Habit)**
Regular, low-cost in-person research — not just scheduled usability studies — done as a "quick, short, sharp, regular sniff test." The method: take one or two people out into the field for a day with minimal setup, observe users with your product in their real environment (at a bus stop, in their home, on the move), and bring back themes and stories. Key questions to ask when observing: Can it be used with one hand? In a low-data environment? On the move? As an interruptible task? If any answer is no, the product fails in the real world. Complements dashboard data by answering *why*, not just *what*.

**The Two-Clicks Problem (Behavioral Data Blindness)**
Identical behavioral signals (e.g., five app opens per day) can represent radically different user experiences and emotional states. Dashboards cannot distinguish between a user casually checking before a discretionary purchase and a user anxiously monitoring every transaction to avoid overdraft charges. Quantitative data needs qualitative context to be interpreted correctly — the framework is a reminder that completion rates and engagement metrics are necessary but not sufficient.
