# How to position your product for a future nobody can predict - April Dunford

Video ID: `Ej_wbIW-Sdw`

## Summary
April Dunford, a positioning expert with 25 years in B2B tech marketing and author of *Obviously Awesome*, discusses how companies should position their products during periods of massive market upheaval — particularly the current AI revolution. Her core argument is that strong positioning requires first identifying your differentiated value, then constructing a compelling, honest point of view about the future that places your product at the center. The talk covers how to communicate that vision without stalling sales, how to keep positioning fresh over time, and how to build systematic feedback loops with customers. It is most relevant to founders, CEOs, product leaders, and product marketers at B2B tech companies navigating AI disruption.

---

## Key insights
- **Positioning is context-setting, not just branding.** It answers the customer's big questions: What is this? Who is it for? What do I compare it to? Why does it matter now? Like the opening scene of a movie, it lets customers settle in and pay attention to details.
- **Repositioning changes everything, not just marketing.** Dunford's early career example: repositioning a "souped-up spreadsheet" as an embeddable database for mobile devices changed the sales model (direct sales vs. one-off downloads), pricing (bulk vs. unit), and the entire product roadmap.
- **Every product team has already planted a flag.** By building a roadmap, you have implicitly made a bet about the future. The job is to articulate that bet to customers — not hide it.
- **Companies that fail to communicate their vision cause customers to freeze.** Salesforce's stock took a beating despite strong revenue because they weren't telling a clear story about what CRM looks like in an agentic world. Customers holding 50-seat contracts didn't know if they were making a smart investment.
- **A point of view on the future must center your strengths.** OpenAI frames the future as "we're the electric company — usage metered, universal." Anthropic frames it as B2B-first, developer-centric. Replit says SaaS will be vibe-coded away on demand. Satya Nadella argues models are a commodity and context (living in SharePoint, email, docs) is the moat. Each vision puts the speaker's core strength at the center.
- **Differentiated value must be locked down before you can build a future narrative.** Most companies struggle to articulate a point of view because they haven't reached internal agreement on what only they can deliver. Get that tight first; then the future story writes itself.
- **Hyping features too far out stalls deals.** If you tell customers "we'll have an amazing AI-native version in two years," they'll say "great, come back in two years." Future-oriented messaging must be rooted in what the product does today.
- **Maturity models bridge the present and the future.** Rather than jumping from "no agents" to "all agents," give customers a step-by-step pathway: model your processes → identify low-hanging fruit for agents → run with human-in-the-loop → gradually automate exception handling → full agentic operation. Each step delivers value now and reduces fear.
- **Positioning needs a regular cadence — every six months.** Markets move, competitors catch up, new capabilities ship. A disciplined checkpoint prevents positioning from going stale without anyone noticing.
- **The sales team is an underused early warning system.** They see new competitors appearing on shortlists months before it shows up in lost deal data. Not systematically capturing that intelligence is a structural failure, not a sales problem.
- **Customer Advisory Boards surface what public data cannot.** You can mine reviews for voice-of-customer language, but you cannot learn from public sources whether the purchase process has changed, how budget is being reallocated, or whether there's a new AI strategic initiative inside an account. Direct, systematic customer conversations are the only way.
- **Intercom/Fin is held up as a strong case study** — they rearchitected from first principles around the core job-to-be-done ("resolve a ticket"), priced around outcomes, and went all-in on AI early. Acquired by Salesforce for ~$3.5B.
- **Stack Overflow is a cautionary tale of silence.** Whether or not the business is actually healthy, the absence of a public narrative about their AI-era positioning made the market assume they were dead.
- **Positioning checkpoints follow the same process each time:** Who are the competitive alternatives? What capabilities do we have that are differentiated? What value themes does that map to? Who cares most about that value? What market category does that put us in? If answers haven't changed, nothing needs updating. If they have, reposition accordingly.

## Use cases
- **B2B SaaS founders** unsure how to respond publicly to the "can't you just vibe code this?" objection from prospects and investors.
- **Product marketers** tasked with refreshing messaging during a period of AI disruption without causing deals to stall.
- **CEOs and exec teams** who need to give a compelling future-state narrative to enterprise customers who are nervous about continued investment.
- **Product managers** building roadmaps who need to align internal teams on differentiated value before setting direction.
- **Revenue/sales leaders** trying to explain to their team why certain deals are freezing and what story to tell.
- **Companies in "legacy" categories** (traditional SaaS, on-prem, etc.) that need to articulate why they still matter in an AI-native world.
- **Companies running customer advisory boards or QBRs** who want a structured agenda beyond relationship maintenance.
- **Startup teams post-pivot** who need to think through what repositioning actually changes (pricing, sales motion, roadmap) — not just the name.

## Patterns & frameworks

**1. The Positioning Definition**
*"Positioning defines how our product is the best in the world at delivering some value that a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about."*
Operationally: identify competitive alternatives → list differentiated capabilities → map to value themes → identify who cares most about that value → determine the market category that frames the comparison correctly.

**2. The Six-Month Positioning Checkpoint**
A recurring workshop cadence (full process every 6 months, speedrun subset in between) that asks: Have competitors changed? Have our differentiated capabilities changed? Has the value mapping changed? If yes to any, reposition. If no, validate and move on. Prevents positioning from silently going stale.

**3. The Maturity Model / Pathway**
A staged roadmap communicated to customers that shows: here is where you are today → here is step 1 (low risk, human-in-the-loop) → step 2 → step 3 → here is the full future state. Solves the "future hype stalls deals" problem by giving customers a reason to buy now and a credible path forward. Each step must deliver standalone value.

**4. The Future Point of View Framework**
To develop a credible market narrative: (a) get ruthlessly clear on your core differentiated value, (b) ask whether that value grows or shrinks in importance in 5 years, (c) construct a vision of the future where that value is central and growing, (d) make sure that vision explains why customers should invest now rather than wait. The vision is not neutral — it should put your strengths at the center.

**5. Systematic Customer Intelligence Loops**
Three mechanisms described: (1) Customer Advisory Boards for strategic questions about budget, purchase process, and priorities; (2) Executive Sponsorship Programs where each exec owns quarterly calls with a handful of accounts; (3) Sales Team Debriefs as a structured early-warning system for competitive shifts. All three are framed as necessary systems, not ad hoc outreach, especially during fast-moving markets.
