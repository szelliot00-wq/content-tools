# The three sacred cows of product leadership that are holding you back (Emily Andrews)

Video ID: `IK7BI_tJlOY`

## Summary
Emily Andrews, a 15-year product veteran, challenges three widely-held product management beliefs she calls "sacred cows" that, while principled in theory, often create territorial behavior and organizational friction in practice. Her core argument is that product leaders in complex organizations should shift from seeking ownership and control to creating conditions for better decisions — becoming "decision architects" rather than decision owners. She draws on real examples from cross-functional team dynamics to show how clinging to product orthodoxy (who owns prioritization, who speaks for the customer, whether decision rights solve friction) can actually reduce product's impact. The talk is most relevant to senior product managers, directors, and VPs navigating large, matrixed organizations where textbook product conditions rarely exist.

## Key insights
- **Product purity is a trap.** Applying product management principles too rigidly in complex orgs turns ownership into territory and principle into dogma — teams start defending the method rather than chasing outcomes.
- **Prioritization ownership breeds conflict, not clarity.** When product insists it "owns" prioritization, disagreements with partner functions become existential ("am I even doing real product?") rather than productive.
- **The shift: decision owner → decision architect.** Product's job is not to win the decision but to prevent an uninformed one — making trade-offs explicit, connecting business value to strategy, and defining success metrics collaboratively.
- **No single function owns the full customer picture.** In complex orgs, marketing, ops, engineering, and product each hold partial truths. Product's unique value is translating and connecting all three lenses (customer truth, business reality, tech possibility) into a coherent, objective view.
- **Decision rights (RACIs) solve the wrong problem.** RACIs clarify accountability but cannot manufacture shared context, good judgment, or trust. When a team reaches for a RACI, they're usually trying to fix a culture/trust problem with a governance tool.
- **Low trust → more process, not better decisions.** Organizations compensate for low trust with more governance, more forums, more approvals, more slides. High trust enables faster, lighter decision-making.
- **Trust is built on competence, delivery, and transparency** — including being transparent when things go badly. Andrews describes earning a skeptical operations leader's trust not by being promoted or proven right, but by learning what that leader was accountable for and consistently delivering.
- **The "given/get" trade-off:** Product gives up obsession with sole roadmap ownership and stops spending political capital on being right. In return, it gains earlier involvement, broader context, greater influence, and shared accountability for outcomes.
- **Effective over right, every time.** Shared OKRs and KPIs with partner functions drive alignment and make outcomes stronger — shared accountability is not a failure of product ownership.
- **Craft still comes first.** None of this means lowering the bar. Strong product craft (discovery, problem framing, quality, disciplined execution) earns credibility, credibility creates influence, and influence expands organizational impact.
- **Product leaders become business leaders with high technical acumen.** The role doesn't shrink — it evolves and grows broader. The bar doesn't drop; it rises.
- **Control is not the proof of product leadership.** Product leadership is proven when great outcomes happen even when product is not in control.

## Use cases
- A product leader struggling with cross-functional alignment in a large, matrixed organization (retail, financial services, enterprise tech, etc.)
- A PM or director whose team is experiencing "roadmap ownership" conflicts with marketing, operations, or business partners
- An org that keeps building RACIs to resolve prioritization disputes but finds morale or decision quality declining anyway
- A product leader who has been handed a solution by a senior stakeholder and wants a framework for getting invited into the problem earlier
- A new product leader joining a company where trust and credibility haven't been established yet with peer functions
- Any product team where members are questioning whether they're "doing real product" because they don't have full decision authority
- Leaders trying to evolve product's organizational role from an execution function to a strategic, cross-functional influence function
- Teams preparing for a roadmap planning cycle and wanting to run it more collaboratively rather than in a "black box"

## Patterns & frameworks

**The Three Sacred Cows**
The organizing structure of the talk — three widely-held product beliefs Andrews argues are harmful when applied rigidly:
1. *Product should own prioritization* → creates territorial behavior
2. *Product should be the voice of the customer* → suggests product owns the only truth that matters
3. *Clear decision rights solve organizational friction* → governance tools can't fix trust problems

**Decision Architect (vs. Decision Owner)**
A mental model for reframing product's role. Instead of owning and winning decisions, the product leader creates the conditions for a good decision: surfacing customer evidence, making trade-offs explicit, connecting business value to strategy, and defining how success is measured — in partnership with others. The goal is to prevent an *uninformed* decision, not to control the outcome.

**The Three-Lens Translation Model**
Product's unique value in a cross-functional room is connecting three lenses that otherwise compete:
- **Customer truth** — needs, behaviors, evidence
- **Business reality** — P&L accountability, KPIs, growth commitments, risk
- **Tech possibility** — feasibility, scalability, what's buildable

Product abstracts the complexity across all three into something coherent and actionable, enabling a better collective decision.

**The Given/Get Trade-off**
A reframe for what product gains by releasing control:
- *Give up:* sole roadmap ownership, political capital spent on being right
- *Get:* earlier involvement in shaping initiatives, broader organizational context, greater influence, shared accountability for outcomes

**Craft → Credibility → Influence → Impact (Flywheel)**
A progression Andrews describes for expanding product's organizational role: strong product craft earns credibility with partners; credibility creates influence beyond the product team; influence expands the scope of impact across the business.

**The Mirror (Self-Diagnostic Questions)**
A set of reflective prompts Andrews offers for product leaders to audit their own orientation:
- Protecting the roadmap, or protecting the outcome?
- Defending the method, or adapting it to the problem?
- Seeking authority, or improving the collective decision?
- Measuring product maturity by control, or by impact and outcomes?
