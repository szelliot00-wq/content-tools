# Your roadmap isn't a strategy: how to build one that sticks

Video ID: `rW3j_nAE-DU`

## Summary
Andrew Scotsko, author of *The Strategy Cycle*, joins Randy Silver on *The Product Experience* podcast to address a pervasive problem: most organizations claim to have a strategy but actually have something else entirely — a roadmap, a plan, goals, or a budget. Andrew defines strategy as "an evolving theory of winning" and walks through his three-phase Strategy Cycle (Craft, Deploy, Manage). The conversation covers why strategy is hard (it's a mental, social, *and* emotional challenge), how to avoid common traps, and where AI and consultants can and cannot help. It is most relevant to product leaders, CPOs, and anyone accountable for product direction and outcomes.

---

## Key insights
- **Most "strategies" are counterfeits.** Roadmaps, plans, goals, budgets, and mission statements are all commonly mistaken for strategy. A real strategy is an *evolving theory of winning* — a causal explanation of how you get customers to choose you, repeatedly.
- **The core definition has three parts:** "Theory" = a causal A→B explanation. "Winning" = customers choose you over alternatives and keep choosing you. "Evolving" = it must change as the market and context change.
- **Alternative framing:** Strategy is "the art of using what we control to impact what we don't control" — namely, customer behavior and outcomes.
- **Strategy's job is to maximize your odds, not guarantee success.** The three things it should deliver: (1) the product wins, (2) teams make good decisions when you're not in the room, (3) you hit goals without destroying your people.
- **Product strategy vs. business strategy:** They're different but should be nested. Product strategy powers the business strategy. In early-stage or scaleup companies, the two heavily overlap. Critically, it's very common for there to be *no* business strategy at all — "grow 40%" is not a strategy.
- **Who owns it:** Whoever has accountability for the product's direction and results. Strategy is better as a team sport — work with a small cross-functional "strategy squad."
- **Always start with the implicit strategy.** Before creating anything new, sketch out what you're *actually* doing today (even if it's never been written down), because strategy is what you do, not what you say.
- **The strategy sketch:** A quick (~1 hour solo, ~2 hours with a team) exercise to explore multiple possible strategic directions at a high level. It surfaces gaps and open questions, showing you where to focus deeper work. Always do at least two sketches — one for the implicit current strategy, one or more for alternatives.
- **The first full Craft phase takes 1 week to 1 month.** Subsequent quarterly iterations are much faster (a couple of days), because the flywheel is already spinning.
- **The strategy cycle runs on a ~quarterly cadence** with a 12–18 month time horizon — zoomed out several levels above build-measure-learn, which runs in days or weeks on features/experiments.
- **Manage phase = three parallel activities:** (1) Support decisions, (2) Stay on course (fight drift), (3) Sense and learn.
- **Drift is the enemy.** The gap between stated intention and lived reality silently kills strategies. Fighting drift is tireless and thankless but may be the most important management activity.
- **Three tactics to sustain the strategy:** Beat the drum constantly (every all-hands, every 1:1, every leadership meeting — it's a campaign, not an event). Protect focus (make choices, fight shiny objects). Correct drift (at least one or two formal checkpoints per quarter).
- **On saying no:** Reframe it as "not now." Use the time horizon — within a quarter, every organization is an "or" company. This makes refusal easier to swallow. Normalize the discomfort; making choices under uncertainty is *supposed* to feel uncomfortable.
- **Strategy is hard on three dimensions, not one:** Most people only think about the *mental* (analytical, frameworks) challenge. But the *emotional* challenge (tolerating uncertainty and the discomfort of hard choices) and the *social* challenge (bringing people along so their choices change) are what kill most strategies. The mental game is actually the easiest of the three.
- **Five uncomfortable truths about strategy:** (1) You don't control customer choice. (2) You can't predict the future. (3) You can't know the best strategy in advance — only in hindsight, and even then only "good enough." (4) You have to say no to good ideas, often. (5) Even doing everything right, there's no guarantee — only better odds.
- **You can't outsource strategy to a consultant or AI.** If the operating leaders don't author and own the choices, the strategy won't stick — it gets "rejected" like a transplanted organ. The choices themselves cannot be delegated.
- **AI's legitimate role in strategy:** Gathering unstructured context (Slack, email, docs), data analysis, finding patterns, pressure-testing, and editing your thinking. Rule 1: retain authorship — own the choices. Rule 2: write the first draft yourself. "LLMs are phenomenal editors and terrible ghost writers."
- **The ghost-written strategy trap:** One client fed their top 50 priorities into an AI and got back a beautifully written document that made *no actual choices*. It sounded strategic but said nothing. Six weeks later, it had completely vanished from practice.
- **The three biggest strategy mistakes:** (1) Confusing a plan/roadmap with a strategy (accepting a counterfeit). (2) Refusing to choose. (3) Not bringing stakeholders along throughout the process — alignment is a through-line, not a sign-off event at the end.
- **Alignment must be continuous.** Waiting until the end for buy-in from the CEO or key stakeholders is a common and devastating failure. Build alignment throughout the crafting process.

## Use cases
- A product leader who has been told to "be more strategic" but has never seen a concrete example of what that means
- A CPO or director building a strategy for the first time and unsure where to start
- A team that keeps getting pulled off-course by leadership requests ("can't you just add X?")
- An organization where the roadmap *is* the strategy and no one has questioned it
- A product team operating inside a company with no coherent business strategy
- A leader trying to get cross-functional alignment on direction without it falling apart in execution
- Anyone evaluating how to responsibly use AI tools in their strategy process
- Scale-ups where product strategy and business strategy need to be better differentiated and connected
- Leaders managing quarterly planning cycles who want a repeatable process rather than a one-time exercise

## Patterns & frameworks

**The Strategy Cycle (3 phases)**
A repeatable quarterly loop consisting of:
1. **Craft** — Develop the strategy. First time takes 1 week–1 month; subsequent iterations take ~2 days. Starts with a strategy sketch.
2. **Deploy** — Bring the strategy to life inside the organization.
3. **Manage** — Run the strategy through the quarter: support decisions, stay on course (fight drift), and sense/learn. Runs ~10–12 weeks per quarter.
Then repeat with learnings fed back into the next Craft phase.

**The Strategy Sketch**
A fast (~1 hour solo) exploratory exercise done before deep strategy work. Always do at least two: one for the current implicit strategy, one or more for alternatives. Surfaces gaps and weak points so you know where to invest deeper effort.

**The Strategy Squad**
A small cross-functional working group (not a solo effort) that the accountable leader assembles to go through the strategy cycle together. Others are consulted and informed, but this squad does the core work.

**Implicit Strategy First**
Before creating anything new, map what the organization is *actually* doing today as a strategy sketch. Strategy is what you do, not what you say — so there's always an implicit one, written down or not.

**The 3D Strategy Challenge (Mental / Emotional / Social)**
Strategy is difficult along three dimensions simultaneously:
- *Mental*: analytical rigor, frameworks, creative thinking under uncertainty
- *Emotional*: tolerating ambiguity, accepting that discomfort is inherent and correct
- *Social*: changing other people's choices; alignment as a continuous through-line, not a final event
Most frameworks address only the mental dimension. The emotional and social dimensions are where most strategies actually fail.

**The Five Uncomfortable Truths**
Gravity-level facts that must be accepted (not solved): no control over customer choice, no ability to predict the future, no way to know the best strategy in advance, mandatory "no" to good ideas, and no guarantees even when you do everything right.

**AI Rules for Strategy (two rules)**
1. Retain authorship — you must own the choices; don't outsource them to AI.
2. Write the first draft yourself — use AI as an editor, not a ghost writer.

**"Not Now" Reframe for Saying No**
When stakeholders resist trade-offs, shift from "no" to "not this quarter." Ground it in time horizon: within a quarter everyone is an "or" company (finite people and money); over a longer horizon, more things become possible. Requires a robust strategic rationale to back it up.

**Beat the Drum / Protect Focus / Correct Drift (Managing the Strategy)**
Three tactics for sustaining strategy in the Manage phase:
- *Beat the drum*: Reinforce strategy at every opportunity — it's a campaign, not an announcement.
- *Protect focus*: Actively resist shiny objects and "can't you just" requests at the leadership level.
- *Correct drift*: Hold at least one or two formal checkpoints per quarter to identify and reverse deviation from the strategy.
