# Fireside Chat with Marty Cagan and Elias Lieberich - ProductTank Berlin

Video ID: `5L41y0ms7Aw`

## Summary
This is a fireside chat from ProductTank Berlin between product veteran Marty Cagan (SVPG, author of *Inspired* and *Empowered*) and product coach Elias Lieberich, recorded in 2026. The conversation covers how AI is transforming product management, delivery, and strategy — drawing parallels to the internet era — while arguing that the craft of product discovery and the role of product leadership are becoming *more* important, not less, as software delivery becomes increasingly automated. Cagan also reflects candidly on what he got wrong in his books, particularly around product leadership and business viability. The talk is most relevant to product managers, product leaders, designers, and engineers navigating the AI transition in both startup and enterprise contexts.

---

## Key insights

- **AI adoption mirrors the internet era, but faster.** Cagan compares the current AI wave to his time at Netscape — daily new technologies, constant "what is this for?" questions, and a race not to be left behind. The key difference: companies are adopting AI significantly faster than they adopted the internet, though actual organizational impact lags stated intent.

- **The AI productivity paradox.** McKinsey named something everyone has observed: teams using AI tools go faster (output increases), but outcomes don't improve proportionally. Speed without direction is the core failure mode. 89% of CEOs expect AI to make their companies faster; very few see meaningful outcome improvement.

- **Two models of building product: project vs. product.** The project model optimizes for predictability — roadmaps of features with dates, PRDs, and output-focused delivery. The product model optimizes for outcomes and innovation. Companies in the project model risk automating away product managers and designers entirely, since those roles in that context are largely administrative and can be replaced by agents. Companies in the product model will see those roles become *more* valuable.

- **Delivery is becoming a software factory; discovery is where humans win.** Anthropic and OpenAI no longer treat delivery as a process (no standups, no sprint planning, no product owners) — they treat it as an automated factory. What remains irreplaceable: deciding *what* to build, strategy, and the craft of product discovery.

- **The three roles are not merging — they're each becoming more specialized.** Despite the "product creator" framing, Cagan is explicit: product managers, designers, and engineers bring distinct lenses. Product managers own business viability (sales channels, legal, compliance, monetization, financials). Designers own holistic user experience. Engineers own technical feasibility and architecture. True "triple threats" who excel at all three are extraordinarily rare — Cagan has met a handful in 45 years.

- **Cagan's biggest regret: not covering product leadership in *Inspired*.** CEOs bought *Inspired* for their teams, read it, and concluded that simply forming empowered teams would produce results. They missed that empowered teams require *better* leadership, not less. This created a pattern — especially in Europe — of teams going in all directions with no strategic alignment. "Empowered teams don't need less leadership, they need better leadership."

- **Cagan's second major regret: underemphasizing business viability.** *Inspired*'s subtitle ("products customers love") caused PMs to fixate on customer value while neglecting the business viability dimension — understanding marketing costs, sales channels, compliance, legal risks, monetization, and runtime costs. This is the core differentiator between a product owner and a real product manager.

- **Product strategy is the product leader's primary responsibility.** Leaders choose which problems to solve; teams solve them. A common dysfunction is product managers being "drawn like a magnet" to problem discovery (validating which problem matters) when their time should be overwhelmingly in solution discovery (figuring out how to solve the assigned problem better than competitors).

- **AI as a personal product coach is a game-changer, and it's free.** Configure your preferred LLM with your company's strategic context — product vision, strategy, operating model — and use it 24/7 for coaching on business viability, compliance, opportunity solution trees, KPIs, and product frameworks. Cagan calls this probably his favorite application of AI for product people. No paid app required.

- **Competitive intensity has dramatically increased.** Cheaper, faster delivery means more entrants in every market. Cagan admits he hasn't said clearly enough in his writing: building products for the market isn't polite problem-solving — it's "a full-contact blood sport." It's not enough to solve a problem well; you must solve it *better than everyone else* to win switching behavior.

- **AI and discovery: build-to-learn is better than ever.** Tools like Lovable are designed for rapid prototyping in discovery (not production). Product managers and designers can now build prototypes themselves without depending on engineers, enabling faster customer testing and iteration. The distinction between a discovery prototype and a production product remains critical — many teams haven't learned this yet.

- **Product leadership's role in the AI era: strategy and judgment.** AI helps leaders analyze data and communicate strategy narratives, but the core of strategy — choosing which bets to make, setting product vision, making team topology decisions — is judgment-based and remains human. AI contributes "on the margin" here.

- **The middle management flattening is real but temporary.** Companies that removed management layers are already starting to rehire. The real driver was leaders needing to get hands-on with AI tools to lead credibly ("experts lead experts" is religion at top companies). But the need for leadership — product vision, strategy, team topology decisions, coaching — doesn't disappear. Span of control may increase as AI takes over one-on-one coaching tasks, but leadership itself remains essential.

- **Tech debt is finally solvable.** Companies are replatforming in 2–3 months what previously took 2–3 years, thanks to AI-assisted engineering. Cagan argues this is one of the best uses of tokens for companies with legacy architecture, and that fixing tech debt unlocks better team topology and autonomy.

- **Outcome-based pricing is accelerating.** Examples: Sierra AI (customer success agents, ~$250M ARR, prices by outcome), Palantir (bets on outcomes with customers, shared upside model, already at a multiple of Accenture's valuation). The economics of AI products — token costs at runtime — are forcing a rethink of flat SaaS pricing. Most companies' practical near-term solution: enable agentic use of their services but let customers or SIs run (and pay for) the agents.

- **Conventional vs. AI (probabilistic) products.** The vast majority of teams are using AI to build faster, but their products remain deterministic/conventional. A small subset is building truly probabilistic AI products. Those are significantly harder to build, and many current AI product efforts are primarily for marketing/investor optics. Evals are the discovery process for probabilistic products — continuous, not one-time.

- **Creativity and AI: the mediocrity trap.** Models are trained on averages and push toward mediocrity. The differentiator remains human judgment and craft — the product designer's eye for what's exceptional vs. adequate. Katie Dill (Head of Design, Stripe) gave what Cagan calls the best answer he's heard on this topic (referenced as a forthcoming Lenny's Podcast talk).

- **Engineers in discovery: half an hour a day matters enormously.** Engineers' primary job is build-to-earn, but 30 minutes of daily engagement with build-to-learn prototypes yields outsized returns: they catch technically ill-advised directions early and often generate the best solution ideas. Elias notes his biggest product wins came from engineer ideas — especially after engineers witnessed real customer behavior firsthand.

- **Documentation now lives in the code.** Top AI companies have moved to embedding all documentation directly in the codebase so language models can parse and use it. This eliminates a long-standing overhead and enables teams to start from a much higher shared cognitive baseline.

## Use cases

- **Product managers in the project model** who want to understand the risk AI poses to their role and how to migrate toward higher-value work (strategy, business viability, discovery).
- **Product leaders** who need to understand their specific responsibility (problem selection, strategy) vs. the team's responsibility (problem solving), especially if their teams are drifting without strategic direction.
- **Product managers seeking to self-assess** and identify skill gaps — particularly around business viability (compliance, legal, sales channels, monetization, financial modeling).
- **Engineers** wondering where they fit as delivery gets automated — understanding the "build-to-earn vs. build-to-learn" distinction and the value of engaging in discovery.
- **Designers** looking to understand why their role is growing in importance as AI fills the market with mediocre outputs and differentiation through craft becomes a competitive advantage.
- **Companies evaluating whether to build AI (probabilistic) products** vs. using AI to build conventional products faster — helping them make a realistic assessment of readiness and ROI.
- **B2B SaaS companies** facing pressure to shift from seat-based to outcome-based pricing and needing a practical architecture strategy (enable agentic use; let customers run/pay for the agents).
- **Product leaders in Europe or risk-averse organizations** who need to understand why cultural preference for predictability and planning conflicts with the product model, and what to do about it.
- **Teams with significant technical debt** looking for a framework to justify and execute a replatforming initiative using AI-assisted engineering.
- **Companies trying to adopt the product model** but lacking leaders who've worked that way — using an AI product coach configured with company strategy as a scalable, free alternative.
- **Internal product managers building probabilistic products** (e.g., HR AI tools in regulated environments) who need to think through evals, guardrails, and continuous discovery.
- **Anyone evaluating team topology changes** as AI makes smaller teams capable of wider end-to-end product scope.

## Patterns & frameworks

**Project Model vs. Product Model**
The central organizing framework of the talk. The project model drives everything from feature roadmaps with dates, optimizing for predictability and output. The product model drives everything from outcomes and innovation, optimizing for solving problems better than competitors. Most companies globally still use the project model; AI accelerates the obsolescence of product management roles within it while amplifying their value in the product model.

**Build to Learn vs. Build to Earn** (Jeff Patton)
Product discovery = build to learn (create prototypes to test ideas, validate solutions, eliminate risk before committing). Product delivery = build to earn (create production software that runs the business). Gen AI has made build-to-learn accessible to non-engineers for the first time, changing who can participate in discovery. These are fundamentally different activities, different tools, different mindsets — conflating them is a major failure mode.

**The Software Factory**
How frontier AI companies (Anthropic, OpenAI) now conceptualize delivery: not a process with ceremonies (standups, sprint planning, retrospectives), but an automated pipeline — code generation, testing, deployment, and monitoring handled by agents. This framework implies the product process question shifts from "how do we run delivery well?" to "what do we automate, and what do humans decide?"

**Business Viability as a Distinct Lens**
Product managers' core and unique contribution to a product team is not user empathy (designer's domain) or technical feasibility (engineer's domain) but business viability: marketing costs and strategy, sales channels and capabilities, go-to-market approach, financial modeling, compliance constraints, legal and privacy risks, monetization strategy, and runtime costs. This is what justifies the product manager role; it cannot be automated in the product model.

**Problem Discovery vs. Solution Discovery**
Product leaders are responsible for problem discovery (identifying which problems are most important to solve, given the full strategic picture). Product teams/managers are responsible for solution discovery (figuring out how to solve the assigned problem in a way that customers love, works for the business, and beats competitors). A common dysfunction is PMs spending their time trying to validate the problem rather than solving it.

**Experts Lead Experts**
A leadership principle described as "religion" at top companies (Apple, Amazon): managers must be practitioners of what they manage. You cannot credibly lead engineers without engineering expertise, or product teams without deep product knowledge. In the AI era, this means every leader must get hands-on with AI tools before they can make sound decisions about AI strategy and team structure.

**AI as Product Coach**
Configure a large language model with your company's strategic context (product vision, product strategy, target problems, operating model) and use it as a 24/7 personal product coach. Effective for: coaching business viability, working through opportunity solution trees, interpreting KPIs, preparing for stakeholder challenges. Free, scalable, and — as of roughly a year before the talk — at an inflection point of quality sufficient to be "at least as good as a reasonably skilled manager" at coaching the product model.

**Outcome-Based vs. Output-Based Pricing**
A business model pattern where companies (Sierra AI, Palantir) charge customers based on results delivered rather than seats or usage. Works when the product solves a problem with a clear, measurable cost (e.g., cost per customer service call). Enabled by AI agents that can demonstrate clear outcome causation. Practical B2B architecture answer for most companies: enable agentic access to your services (via platform APIs), but let customers or SIs run and pay for the agents rather than absorbing the token cost yourself.

**Conventional vs. Probabilistic Products**
Conventional/deterministic products: no per-inference runtime cost, predictable behavior, zero marginal cost at scale — still the majority of what teams build. Probabilistic/AI products: non-deterministic outputs, significant token costs at runtime, require continuous evals as the discovery mechanism. These are harder to build and justify, and many current AI product efforts are performative rather than substantive.

**Evals as Continuous Product Discovery**
For probabilistic AI products, evals (evaluation frameworks that test model outputs against desired behaviors) are not a one-time QA step but an ongoing discovery-and-delivery loop. Guardrails, edge cases, and failure modes must be continuously monitored and updated — particularly critical in sensitive domains (HR, regulated industries). Teresa Torres's recent writing cited as the best primer on this.

**Triple Threat**
A practitioner who is genuinely expert in all three product disciplines: product management (business viability, strategy), design (service design, interaction design, visual design, user research), and engineering (architecture, fault tolerance, performance, token cost management). Extraordinarily rare — Cagan has met a handful in 45 years. Not a scalable strategy; teams must be built to provide complementary coverage across the three lenses.
