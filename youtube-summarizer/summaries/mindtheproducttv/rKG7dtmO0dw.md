# How to build emotional intelligence and resilience  - Pippa Topp (CPO, giffgaff)

Video ID: `rKG7dtmO0dw`

## Summary
Pippa Topp, CPO of Giffgaff, discusses emotional intelligence (EI) with hosts Lily Smith and Randy Silver, covering what EI is, how it manifests in product management, and how to develop it in yourself and your team. The core argument is that self-awareness — understanding the stories you tell yourself versus observable facts — is the foundation of EI, and that building this skill directly improves your ability to lead, communicate, and navigate organizational politics. Pippa draws on her own journey from being judgmental and over-competitive to over-empathetic to finding a balanced, compassionate-but-accountable leadership style. The episode also touches on neurodivergence, AI's role in EI, and whether crying at work is acceptable. It is most relevant to product managers, product leaders, and anyone in a cross-functional role who must manage stakeholders and teams.

## Key insights
- **EI defined**: Emotional intelligence is understanding how you feel, how others feel, and regulating your behavior in response — drawing on Daniel Goleman's foundational work.
- **Defensiveness is the most visible EI failure point**: When stakeholders ask questions that feel threatening (e.g., asking for a feature instead of focusing on outcomes), product managers often react defensively rather than getting curious. This is a signal that unchecked emotion is driving communication.
- **The stimulus-response gap is the core skill**: Building space between a triggering event and your response — by pausing to ask "what story am I telling myself?" and "what are the actual facts?" — is the practical mechanism of EI in action.
- **EI parallels customer research skills**: Just as PMs practice empathy mapping and reading between the lines with customers, those same skills can and should be applied to stakeholders and peers. Most PMs don't make this transfer consciously.
- **Unconscious incompetence is the starting problem**: People who lack EI often don't know they lack it. Coaching someone out of this requires first making their blind spots visible — through life story reflection, observation of real-time reactions, and tools like behavioral assessments.
- **The life story exercise**: Pippa asks coaching clients to tell their life story. The process itself surfaces patterns the person hasn't noticed — e.g., someone who believes they're bad at starting new things but has actually moved countries and started businesses multiple times.
- **Separating facts from narrative**: A key coaching technique is asking someone to describe what actually happened (facts only), then separately articulate the story they layered on top. This prevents spiral thinking and catastrophizing.
- **Pippa's own arc — from judgy to over-empathetic to balanced**: Early in her career she was competitive and judgmental. Postnatal depression led her to therapy, which built empathy. She then overcorrected into affective empathy (feeling everyone's feelings), which paralyzed her leadership. The final stage was compassion combined with accountability.
- **Affective vs. cognitive empathy distinction**: Cognitive empathy (understanding others' perspectives intellectually) is healthy and useful. Affective empathy (taking on others' feelings emotionally) can cripple a leader's ability to hold people accountable or make hard decisions.
- **Forcing a decision the team hates**: Pippa describes leading a team through a mandated roadmap change she couldn't fully explain due to context she couldn't share. Rather than getting defensive at the team's resistance, she named and surfaced all three sources of discomfort: the principle of the decision, the information asymmetry, and the practical frustration of having work added without consent. This allowed the team to "disagree and commit."
- **Team EI predicts team performance**: Lily references a talk by Linda Rising citing research that overall team emotional intelligence is the number one predictor of team success.
- **Building EI culture at the org level**: Pippa runs group coaching sessions at Giffgaff on topics like communication and personal development. She also introduced the "pockets of brilliance" model — you don't need the whole org to be great; you can influence your immediate team by modeling specific values.
- **Measuring EI via behavior mapping**: Map desired values (e.g., curiosity) to concrete observable behaviors (asking for clarity, challenging with kindness) and their opposites (assumption-making, defensiveness, analysis paralysis). These become measurable team norms.
- **Neurodivergence and EI**: Lily shares her own autism diagnosis and explains how understanding her black-and-white thinking style helped her explain her communication style to others proactively, deepening relationships rather than damaging them. Pippa's response: EI is not one-size-fits-all; building a high-EI team means understanding who is actually on your team.
- **AI as an EI tool**: Pippa uses ChatGPT/Gemini to critique her written communications from multiple stakeholder perspectives — a practical use of AI to simulate social awareness before sending important messages.
- **Human connection is irreplaceable**: Pippa references a study on rats given addictive substances — isolated rats became dependent while socially connected rats did not — to argue that connection is biologically essential, and EI is the skill that enables optimal human connection.
- **Crying at work**: Crying is a physiological response and is not inherently unprofessional. The important question is *why* it's happening. Crying while telling a meaningful personal story = fine. Crying in response to being shouted at in a board meeting = a signal of dysregulation on both sides. The root cause of stress-driven crying in leaders is usually a lack of self-belief.
- **Self-belief as the foundation of emotional resilience**: The underlying cause of most leadership stress breakdowns is not the external pressure but the absence of a stable internal belief in one's own capability. Building "I can handle whatever is thrown at me" and "I am not my work" as core beliefs reduces how personally threatening external events feel.
- **Role clarity when someone cries to you**: As a manager you are not a therapist or a savior. Knowing when to listen, when to signpost to a professional, and when to set a boundary is itself an act of EI.

## Use cases
- A product manager who becomes defensive in stakeholder meetings when asked about delivery timelines or feature prioritization.
- A team lead who needs to deliver an unpopular decision (e.g., a forced roadmap change) without destroying trust or triggering team resistance.
- A coach or manager trying to help a junior PM who is resistant to reflective or emotional work but is struggling with confidence and role fit.
- A product leader who has overcorrected into excessive empathy and is finding it hard to hold people accountable or make hard calls.
- An organization wanting to move from ad hoc EI to a team-wide culture of curiosity and psychological safety.
- A leader managing a neurodivergent team member (or who is themselves neurodivergent) and needs to build communication norms that work for everyone.
- A product manager preparing a sensitive all-hands or stakeholder email and wanting to pressure-test its tone and implications before sending.
- Anyone in a leadership role experiencing stress-induced emotional dysregulation who needs to build resilience from the inside out.
- A manager who has had a team member cry or become visibly emotional and is unsure of their role and responsibility in that moment.

## Patterns & frameworks

**Conscious Competence Ladder (Noel Burch, 1970s)**
A four-stage model of skill acquisition: (1) Unconsciously incompetent — you don't know what you don't know; (2) Consciously incompetent — you're now aware of the gap; (3) Consciously competent — you can do the skill but it takes deliberate effort; (4) Unconsciously competent — the skill is automatic. Pippa uses this to explain why coaching has to start by making the invisible visible before improvement is possible.

**Facts vs. Story separation**
A coaching technique where you ask someone to describe an upsetting situation in two separate layers: (1) the observable facts only, and (2) the narrative/interpretation they placed on top. Separating these prevents emotional spiral and reveals where assumptions are driving reactions rather than evidence.

**Stimulus → Space → Response**
The core EI mechanism: between any triggering stimulus and your behavioral response, you can build a gap. The larger that gap, the more choice you have in how to respond. The gap is built through self-awareness practices like pausing, naming the emotion, and questioning the story.

**Pockets of Brilliance (Pippa Topp, Mind the Product 2022)**
A leadership model based on the idea that you don't need organizational-wide change to shift culture — you can create local pockets of high-performance by embodying specific values (curiosity, collaboration, learning, creativity) in your immediate team. Map those values to observable behaviors to make them measurable and actionable.

**Cognitive vs. Affective Empathy distinction**
Cognitive empathy = intellectually understanding someone else's perspective. Affective empathy = emotionally absorbing their feelings as your own. The first is a leadership asset; the second, in excess, is a liability that can undermine accountability and decision-making.

**Life Story Coaching Exercise**
Asking a coaching client to narrate their full life story as a diagnostic tool. The act of telling it surfaces patterns, contradictions, and unacknowledged strengths the client hasn't noticed — creating raw material for building self-awareness without the coach needing to impose an agenda.

**CREDIT Model (Julia Whitney)** — referenced briefly
A model for seeking commonality and reciprocity in difficult stakeholder relationships. Not detailed in the episode but mentioned as a tool for building motivation maps and empathy maps for peers, not just customers.

**Insights Discovery (color-based behavioral assessment)**
A psychometric tool used at Giffgaff during onboarding. Assigns color blends to behavioral profiles and highlights strengths and blind spots. Pippa uses it as an entry point for surfacing "empathy misses" — moments where your own emotional state disconnects you from others (e.g., excessive enthusiasm disengaging a team).
