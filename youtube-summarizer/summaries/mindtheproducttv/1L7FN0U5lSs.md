# Welcome to the software renaissance: Martin Eriksson at Mind the Product Chicago 2026

Video ID: `1L7FN0U5lSs`

## Summary

Martin Eriksson's talk at Mind the Product Chicago 2026 argues that AI is not primarily a feature to bolt onto products or a cost-cutting tool, but a fundamental forcing function that invalidates hidden assumptions buried deep in every company's strategic stack. He introduces the "decision stack" framework (vision → strategy → objectives → opportunities → principles) and contends that AI has broken at least one assumption in every layer of every organization's stack — and most companies are looking in the wrong place. The talk draws on historical analogues (Jevons paradox, Gmail, radiologists) alongside modern case studies (Chegg, IKEA, Intercom) to show that the winners will be those who climb the stack until they find what still holds, then rebuild from there. It is most relevant to product managers, product leaders, and executives navigating AI strategy decisions.

## Key insights

- **We are terrible at predicting the future.** Even Geoffrey Hinton (Nobel laureate, "godfather of deep learning") predicted in 2016 that radiologists would be replaced within 5–10 years. Instead, there are 16% *more* radiologists in the US today, earning 44% more on average — because cheaper, faster imaging unlocked massive new demand (preventative screening at scale).
- **The Jevons Paradox applies to AI.** Economist William Stanley Jevons observed in 1865 that making coal *more efficient* caused coal *consumption* to rise dramatically, not fall. The same dynamic is playing out with AI: making something cheaper and faster causes you to do vastly more of it, not less. This is the core strategic lens for thinking about AI's impact.
- **Every strategic decision rests on hidden assumptions, and AI has invalidated at least one of yours.** The decision stack (vision, strategy, objectives, opportunities, principles) is only as sound as the assumptions beneath each layer. The critical skill is knowing which assumption broke.
- **Most companies are making two anti-patterns:**
  - Treating AI as a **feature** ("AI-powered X") — which assumes the customer's problem is a *lack of AI*. Research from Washington State University shows that the phrase "artificial intelligence" in product descriptions actually *reduces* purchase intent.
  - Treating AI as a **cost-cutting tool** — which assumes demand and value creation are finite. This uses once-in-a-generation technology to shrink rather than grow.
- **Chegg is the cautionary tale.** When ChatGPT launched, Chegg's CEO announced they'd build "Chegg Mate" with OpenAI — treating it as a feature. They never examined the assumption one level up: whether students would continue *paying* for homework answers when ChatGPT offered them for free. Result: stock dropped 50% in one day, revenue fell 75%, subscriber numbers were stopped being reported, and the company faced NYSE delisting as of 2026.
- **IKEA is the cost-cutting counterexample.** When AI resolved 50% of support tickets automatically, the obvious move was to cut 8,000 people. Instead, IKEA analyzed what remained (mostly design questions like "does this curtain match my sofa?") and retrained those employees as an interior design service. That team generated **$1.4 billion in net new revenue** in its first year.
- **Intercom is the strategy-level rethink case study.** When ChatGPT dropped, Intercom scrapped their roadmap by Monday and went all-in on AI. They asked: do AI features matter? No. Does our per-seat model hold? No (fewer humans doing support). Do businesses still want to serve customers? Yes. They rebuilt from that surviving assumption. Their AI agent Finn now resolves over **1 million tickets/week** and hit **$100M ARR in its first year**. They shifted from per-seat pricing to **$0.99 per resolution** — aligning incentives completely. Intercom was valued at $1.3B in 2018 and sold to Salesforce for **$3.6B** shortly before the talk.
- **The "go up until one holds" principle.** Chegg went up zero levels (didn't question any assumption). IKEA went up to strategy (found it held, rebuilt below it). Intercom went up to vision (found it held, rebuilt everything under it). The right move is to climb until you find stable ground.
- **Today's AI is the worst AI we will ever work with.** Model costs are collapsing ~10x year-on-year. This means assumptions based on current AI costs and capabilities are especially fragile.
- **The SaaS-pocalypse is probably overstated.** A third of companies are trying to replace SaaS with internal builds, but two-thirds of internal products fail. Enterprise software spend is growing 15% YoY. Even Anthropic — with the world's best engineers and no token cost concerns — still uses Slack, Workday, and Figma.
- **You can't A/B test the future.** All assumption testing uses existing data, customers, and information. The answer is to plan for *multiple* futures (e.g., tokens go to zero AND tokens go to 10x) and identify which assumptions survive both.
- **Write down the bet.** Using Annie Duke's framing, calling something a "bet" makes the risk explicit and gives you something to return to. You need "kill criteria" — predetermined signals that an assumption has broken. IKEA's was revenue from the new team; Intercom's was resolution rate; Google Gmail's was the price of storage.
- **We are in the DOS era of AI.** The current paradigm (typing prompts into chat interfaces) is analogous to the DOS command-line era. The GUI/mouse was invented by people at Xerox PARC who rejected the assumption that "a computer is a device you type commands into." The strategic opportunity is to be those people.

## Use cases

- **Product managers** reviewing their roadmap and wanting to understand whether AI initiatives are genuinely strategic or just feature-level noise
- **CPOs and product leaders** who need a framework for auditing their product strategy for broken assumptions before a competitor exploits them
- **Executives and board members** asking "what are we doing about AI?" and looking for a structured way to think beyond cost-cutting
- **Teams building AI features** who want to pressure-test whether they are solving a real customer problem or just adding AI for AI's sake
- **Organizations considering workforce reductions** due to AI automation — the IKEA case offers a reframe of what that productivity surplus can be redirected toward
- **SaaS companies** worried about being disrupted by customers building their own tools in-house
- **Strategy and planning teams** running annual planning cycles who need to stress-test assumptions across multiple possible futures
- **Anyone responsible for pricing and business model decisions** — the Intercom per-resolution model illustrates how AI can enable entirely new commercial structures
- **Leaders communicating about AI internally** — the framing of "which assumption broke?" gives a concrete, non-panic-inducing way to structure those conversations

## Patterns & frameworks

**The Decision Stack**
A five-layer hierarchy — Vision → Strategy → Objectives → Opportunities → Principles — where each layer answers "how" (top-down) and "why" (bottom-up). Every layer is a decision about what you will *and won't* do. The key insight is that every layer rests on hidden assumptions, and AI has likely invalidated at least one. The practical use: audit each layer by asking "what assumption underlies this?" and climb until you find the one that still holds.

**The Jevons Paradox (applied to AI)**
Historical economic principle: making a resource more efficient increases total consumption rather than reducing it. Applied here: AI doesn't just let you do the same thing cheaper — it unlocks previously inaccessible demand. The strategic question to ask: *What demand is currently locked up that AI could unlock? What would customers do with your product if it were 10x cheaper, faster, or more accessible?*

**"Go up until one holds"**
A decision rule for how far to climb the stack when re-evaluating strategy. Don't stop at the feature or objective level. Keep questioning each layer's underlying assumptions — going from opportunities to objectives to strategy to vision — until you find a layer whose assumptions survive the AI shift. Rebuild everything below that layer from scratch.

**Roger Martin's "What would have to be true?"**
A question-framing technique for surfacing assumptions at each layer of the stack. Instead of defending a strategy, ask what conditions would need to be true for it to be valid. This makes hidden assumptions explicit and testable.

**Planning for multiple futures**
Because the future is unpredictable (even Nobel laureates get it wrong), strategies should be evaluated against multiple plausible scenarios simultaneously (e.g., "tokens go to zero" vs. "tokens go to 10x"). Assumptions that survive both scenarios are more robust; assumptions that fail either scenario are vulnerabilities.

**Annie Duke's "Kill Criteria" / Bet framing**
Calling a strategic decision a "bet" rather than a "plan" makes the risk explicit and surfaces the implicit assumptions. Writing down the bet — specifically, what would have to be false for this bet to fail — creates kill criteria: predetermined signals that tell you when to change course. Examples: IKEA's kill criterion was whether the redesigned help desk generated new revenue; Intercom's was whether Finn's resolution rate held up.

**The "AI as multiplier" framing (vs. feature or efficiency)**
A three-tier diagnostic for how companies are treating AI:
1. *Feature* — sprinkling AI into products; solves no real problem
2. *Efficiency/cost-cutting* — using AI to do the same thing cheaper; assumes finite demand
3. *Multiplier* — rethinking strategy, business model, and what's now *possible* that wasn't before

The argument is that only the third tier creates durable competitive advantage.
