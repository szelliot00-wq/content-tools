# How to fix your product roadmap — Matt Feczko (Director of Product Ops, Ocado Technology)

Video ID: `a9-eFq3vkD8`

## Summary
Matt Feczko, Director of Product and Engineering Ops at Ocado Technology, shares how he diagnosed and fixed systemic product management dysfunction at a 4,000-person company — starting with Jira data hygiene and scaling up to a company-wide skills framework now used by 3,000 employees. The core argument is that product ops functions as "connective tissue" in an organization: it finds tension, solves it systematically, and then moves to the next problem. The video is most relevant to product leaders, product ops practitioners, and senior PMs at scaling companies who are wrestling with roadmap chaos, inconsistent ways of working, and career development frameworks.

## Key insights
- **Two months of three spent on slides:** When Matt joined Ocado, every quarter teams spent two of three months building Google Slides road maps. Actual product work got roughly one month per quarter. The fix was standardizing Jira data so an app could tell the story automatically — he's now aiming to run the next governance cycle with zero slides.
- **The real problem was data, not slides:** The slide chaos was a symptom. The root cause was zero Jira consistency — epics had parent epics had parent epics, and every team used the hierarchy differently. Matt imposed a strict structure ("dictatorial tyrant," his words) backed by the Denise Teal/Melissa Perry *Product Operations* book.
- **Adoption is the hard part, not the solution:** Coming up with the right Jira hierarchy took days; getting ~3,000 people to change their behavior took years and is still ongoing. He frames this explicitly as a classic product problem: how do you drive adoption?
- **Product ops is a force multiplier, not a PMO:** The distinction Matt draws is that product ops people are product people — they identify the actual underlying problem, design a solution, and implement it, rather than just enforcing process or making slides look better.
- **The "Jura Revolution":** Internal change program at Ocado to standardize Jira usage. Named with some flair, it introduced a clear hierarchy: initiatives → features → stories, with consistent definitions enforced across all teams.
- **Metrics must translate to financial value:** Ocado's business goal of reaching cash-flow positive forced the product ops team to partner with Finance to build a metric tree — mapping product metrics (e.g., driver order throughput, basket size) to financial value, so roadmap investments can be articulated in business terms.
- **12-skill framework across 4 themes:** Matt built a PM skills framework with 3 skills per theme, covering execution, influence, strategy, and a fourth theme. Skills include storytelling & evangelizing, problem solving, prioritization, leading with data, domain expertise, and product adoption. Definitions are intentionally specific in intent but flexible in application.
- **Storytelling & evangelizing as the meta-skill:** Matt's personal favorite — he argues it encapsulates all other skills, because if you can tell a compelling story, you've necessarily demonstrated data fluency, domain expertise, prioritization, and vision simultaneously.
- **150 PMs → 3,000 employees adopted the framework:** Engineering, Data, and UX independently saw the product skills framework and adapted it for their own crafts. The four themes and structure (3 skills per theme) stayed the same; the specific skills changed per discipline. Now 3,000 people across Ocado have bespoke versions.
- **Promotions became fairer:** Before the framework, promotion success correlated with manager advocacy skill, not IC performance. With a consistent skills matrix, promotion decisions became more transparent and equitable across the whole organization.
- **Product adoption as a deliberate skill:** Ocado added "product adoption" as an explicit PM skill because PMs believed their job ended at feature delivery — they didn't see driving actual customer adoption as their responsibility. This closed a specific observed gap.
- **Scale threshold for product ops headcount:** Matt suggests ~30–40 product managers before dedicating actual headcount to product ops. Below that, someone can absorb the function part-time. Geographic or cultural distribution can lower that threshold.
- **AI won't change the fundamental skills:** Matt is clear that storytelling, data literacy, and prioritization remain essential regardless of AI. His framing: "AI is just a side bet."
- **Vibe-coded apps replacing slides:** Matt's team has built several internal apps on top of Jira data using AI-assisted coding. He describes eventually wanting a vibe-coded skills assessment app with anonymized benchmarks, trend history, and personalized recommendations pulled from Slack and Google Drive activity.
- **The "Product Day" as a community-building win:** After three years of trying, Matt organized a full product community day hosted by Amazon, bringing together product managers from around the world including UX. Polish PMs — who he describes as emotionally reserved — told him it was the most fun they'd had. He cites this as the clearest proof of value his team has produced.

## Use cases
- **Scaling product teams (30+ PMs):** Any organization growing past the point where informal coordination breaks down — inconsistent Jira usage, fragmented roadmaps, siloed teams.
- **Product ops practitioners justifying their function:** The "connective tissue / fascia" framing and the force-multiplier argument are directly usable when explaining product ops to skeptical stakeholders or CPOs.
- **Building or refreshing a PM skills framework:** Especially relevant if you're at a company where promotion feels political, career paths are unclear, or different teams assess PMs against completely different criteria.
- **Cross-functional skills adoption:** If you're in engineering, data, or design and want a consistent skills/growth framework, Matt's approach of adapting the product framework (keeping themes, swapping specific skills) is a concrete model.
- **Roadmap governance reform:** Teams spending disproportionate time on planning artifacts rather than building — the Ocado example of replacing slides with a live data app is directly applicable.
- **Connecting product metrics to financial value:** Product teams that speak only in metrics (NPS, DAU, conversion) but struggle to connect to P&L or investor-level goals will find the Finance partnership model useful.
- **Remote or geographically distributed product teams:** Matt specifically calls out cultural and geographic distribution as accelerators for needing product ops investment.
- **Community building within product organizations:** The "Product Day" playbook — bringing together a global, siloed product community for a shared learning event — applies to any large org that grew fast through COVID-era remote hiring.

## Patterns & frameworks

**The Connective Tissue Model (Product Ops philosophy)**
Product ops exists to find organizational tension — friction, inefficiency, miscommunication — and resolve it systematically. Once one tension is resolved, the next one surfaces. Matt explicitly says this is not whack-a-mole *if* you fully solve each problem before moving to the next, rather than context-switching constantly.

**Minimum Viable Bureaucracy**
Standardize only the fields and processes that are genuinely non-negotiable for cross-team communication and data integrity. Everything else should remain flexible for individual teams. The question to ask: "What's the minimum that must be consistent so we can tell a coherent story?"

**Jira Hierarchy ("Jura Revolution")**
A strict, company-wide Jira taxonomy: Initiatives → Features → Stories (or equivalent levels). Each level has a clear definition. The key insight from the *Product Operations* book: without a shared hierarchy, data is incomparable and roadmaps are incoherent. The change program had a name, was backed by an external authority (the book), and measured success by adoption rate (they hit 100% completion on the skills assessment).

**The 12-Skill PM Framework (4 themes × 3 skills)**
- Four themes: Execution, Influence, Strategy, and a fourth (implied but not fully enumerated in the transcript).
- Three named skills: Storytelling & Evangelizing, Prioritization, Leading with Data. Others include Domain Expertise, Problem Solving, Product Adoption.
- Assessment method: self-score + manager score, overlaid on a radar chart, used to drive a structured career conversation — not primarily a compensation tool.
- Skills are "specific in intent, flexible in application" — the philosophy of a skill matters more than a universal behavioral rubric.

**Metric Tree → Financial Value Translation**
Product metrics (driver throughput, basket size) are mapped through a metric tree to financial outcomes. Built in partnership with Finance. Enables roadmap investments to be articulated at a business/investor level, not just a product health level. This is the mechanism that lets Okado communicate the *why* behind R&D spend.

**Framework Adoption Playbook**
1. Identify the symptom (bad slides, no roadmap).
2. Diagnose the root cause (no data consistency).
3. Design the solution (Jira hierarchy, skills matrix).
4. Drive adoption relentlessly — treat it like a product launch, not a policy mandate.
5. Let adjacent teams adapt the model for their own craft rather than forcing uniformity.
6. Add tooling and AI augmentation once the foundational model is stable.
