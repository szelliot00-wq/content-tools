# A guide to building a smarter pricing strategy | Carrie Osman (Founder & CEO, Cruxy)

Video ID: `QhvANackM44`

## Summary
Carrie Osman, founder of Cruxy (a B2B tech pricing and product value consultancy), joins Randy Silver to discuss how companies can build smarter pricing strategies grounded in customer value rather than market trends or competitive pressure. The central argument is that pricing is not an isolated decision but the direct manifestation of product value for a specific market segment — and most companies get it wrong by skipping fundamentals, chasing AI premiums, or treating pricing as a sticker-price exercise. The conversation covers how to know when pricing is right, how AI is distorting pricing decisions, the relationship between product packaging and pricing, defensibility in a world of fast copying, and how product managers can integrate pricing thinking into roadmap work. It is most relevant to B2B SaaS product managers, founders, and pricing/product marketing leads working in enterprise or mid-market software.

## Key insights
- **Win rate, churn, NRR, and GRR are the data signals that confirm pricing is right.** A B2B win rate above 30% is the benchmark average. Churn from M&A (not dissatisfaction) and high NRR are signs customers genuinely value the product at its price point.
- **Not all pricing feedback is equal — weight it accordingly.** A senior budget holder with an existing relationship who calls the product underpriced is a "5 out of 5" signal. A new contact who saw one demo and has no budget is a "0." Procurement teams are incentivized to reduce list price and don't understand product value, so their pushback should be heavily discounted.
- **AI is causing companies to skip pricing fundamentals.** Many teams are slapping an "AI" label on existing features and raising prices 30–50%, but this conflates table-stakes product improvements (which customers expect as part of normal 6–8% annual price increases) with genuinely transformative AI capabilities (e.g., compressing a 6-step workflow into 1).
- **Table stakes should be in the base product; premium pricing applies only to features that create distinct, segment-specific uplift.** You cannot charge extra for the product just to function. Pricing tiers and add-ons are only justified for "spikes" — features that take a customer from a 6 to a 9, specific to a cluster of customers with proven willingness to pay.
- **Pricing is the manifestation of product value in a given market for a given customer — it cannot be done in isolation.** Carrie's core thesis is that you cannot price correctly without understanding the market, the customer workflow, and the product's role in that workflow simultaneously.
- **Outcome-based pricing models (e.g., Intercom's Fin shifting from per-seat to success-based) are powerful but must match how buyers actually buy.** Agentic/token-based pricing with 70 variables is not how CFOs and procurement teams sign off on enterprise software. Complexity in pricing models creates friction even if the economic logic is sound.
- **Product marketing and sales enablement are major gaps in pricing execution.** A product can be well-priced but fail because salespeople cannot articulate the ROI, the demo doesn't land the value, or the pitch isn't differentiated by customer segment (a Fortune 500 manufacturer vs. a 100-person Series B cybersecurity firm).
- **Pricing signals quality in B2B, not just B2C.** Large consulting fees (McKinsey, Bain) partly derive credibility from their price. "Free is expensive" in B2B — onboarding, training, tech stack integration, and security review carry real costs even at $0 price points.
- **Going cheap to grab market share requires explicit, upfront communication of the strategy.** Race-to-the-bottom pricing without a clear narrative about why you're affordable undermines perceived product quality. It also doesn't fix the real problem if the team can't sell or explain value.
- **Defensibility is about the product's right to remain in the customer's stack.** The question is: what makes this product irreplaceable even when a competitor can replicate features quickly? Answers include: data network effects, partner channel dominance, bottom-up product-led growth (e.g., Atlassian), customer-obsessed culture, and deep workflow integration.
- **Most product managers have never mapped where their product actually sits in driving last-mile value for customers.** This analysis — overlaying product modules against customer workflow, churn by module, usage by module, service tickets, and tech stack data — is essential to defensibility and pricing work.
- **Curiosity quotient (CQ) is a critical skill for pricing work.** The discipline of drilling past the first 3 layers of a data question — e.g., why does ARR for one customer quartile index at 160 vs. median but those customers churn? — is what surfaces real insight. Most people stop too early.

## Use cases
- **Product managers building or revising a roadmap** who want to attach revenue impact (size of prize) estimates to features before committing to build them.
- **Founders or CPOs pricing a new product or feature tier** who need a framework for deciding what goes in the base vs. premium vs. add-on.
- **Teams evaluating whether to monetize AI features separately** or embed them into existing packages.
- **Sales and revenue leaders** trying to understand why win rates are below 30% or why pricing objections keep surfacing in deals.
- **Customer success and retention teams** diagnosing churn and trying to understand whether it reflects pricing misalignment or product-value gaps.
- **Product marketers** building demos, ROI narratives, or segment-specific sales plays that need to reflect actual customer workflow and value.
- **B2B SaaS companies considering a shift in pricing model** (e.g., per-seat to usage-based or outcome-based) who need to evaluate whether the model matches how buyers actually make decisions.
- **Enterprise software companies** trying to identify which features or modules create defensible lock-in vs. which are easily replicated.

## Patterns & frameworks

**Day-in-the-life (customer-in) workflow mapping**
Borrowed from Carrie's consumer goods background (e.g., studying pet-owner behavior in granular detail), this method involves shadowing or deeply modeling a customer's actual workflow — not how the product team imagines it's used. The goal is to identify friction points, integration dependencies, and "unsaid" needs that customers can't articulate because they can't imagine the solution. Applied in B2B, this means mapping workflow steps, data movement, integrations, and pain points, then overlaying product modules to find gaps, usage anomalies, and churn patterns.

**Weighted signal scoring for pricing feedback**
Rather than treating all market signals equally, score each piece of feedback on a 0–5 scale based on: the seniority and budget authority of the source, their relationship depth with your company, and the specificity and consistency of the feedback. Procurement pushback = low weight. Senior repeat buyer saying you're underpriced = high weight. The goal is to avoid being misled by loud but low-signal noise while catching genuine pricing-fit signals.

**Size-of-prize roadmap assessment**
Every roadmap item should have a rough revenue impact estimate attached before it enters development. This starts as "t-shirt sizing" (order-of-magnitude estimates — thousands, tens of thousands, millions) and tightens as the feature moves toward launch. The estimate should cover: churn mitigation value (with evidence from lost-deal reviews and usage data), win rate improvement for a specific segment, and partner/sales feedback. This builds a compound advantage over time — doing it for 30 minutes per week creates a year's worth of pricing and prioritization intelligence.

**Table stakes / spike / add-on packaging model**
Carrie's approach to packaging mirrors the Kano model but with a commercial lens: the base product must include everything that makes it function well for the ICP (table stakes) — you cannot charge extra for basic functionality. Features that take a specific customer segment from "good enough" to "exceptional" and reflect a distinct willingness to pay can be priced as premium tiers or add-ons. The key test: does the ICP have everything they need in the base product to get genuine value?

**Metal detector metaphor for pricing signals**
A heuristic for navigating ambiguous market feedback: keep sweeping (gathering data from multiple sources — usage, tickets, API volumes, win/loss reviews, NRR trends), and only dig when the detector goes off clearly. When it does, evaluate whether the signal is real (gold) or noise (a tin can). Don't overreact to individual data points; look for triangulated, consistent signals before changing pricing.

**Defensibility stack analysis**
Map the customer's full technology workflow and identify where your product sits relative to last-mile value delivery. Overlay churn by module, usage by module, service tickets, and tech stack data to identify which parts of the product are deeply embedded (hard to replace) vs. peripheral (easy to cut). This analysis reveals where pricing power actually lives and where the product is vulnerable to being "ripped and replaced."
