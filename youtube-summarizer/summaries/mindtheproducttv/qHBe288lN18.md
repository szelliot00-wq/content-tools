# How to prototype fast, test with real data and decide what is worth building | Soufi Esmaeilzadeh

Video ID: `qHBe288lN18`

## Summary
This episode of The Product Experience features Soufi Esmaeilzadeh, Product Director for AI Experiences at Google Search, discussing how she leads the team behind AI Overviews and AI Mode. She covers how search has evolved from 10 blue links to multi-turn AI conversations, and makes the case that this is evolution, not disruption. The core framework throughout is rapid prototyping with real data, hypothesis-driven building, and shipping with a high bar of craft and quality. Most relevant to senior PMs and product leaders navigating AI-era product development, especially in high-scale consumer products.

## Key insights
- **Search is in its biggest evolutionary moment**: The AI paradigm shift has unlocked a new class of problems — things PMs could previously only dream about are now buildable. Consumer behavior has already shifted: users now ask full-sentence questions like "I'm going on a trip with my kid to celebrate his birthday and I'm looking for a kid-friendly hotel" rather than keywords like "hotel Los Angeles."
- **Idea to demo with real data should happen fast**: Soufi explicitly tells her team not to spend weeks debating or building decks. The process should be: define the hypothesis → prototype → demo with real data → beta, all very quickly. She calls this a "scrappy demo — the bare minimum of what you need."
- **Early hypotheses still matter**: Despite the emphasis on speed, she stresses you must ask upfront: "Do I think this is a big opportunity? Will it make a difference in people's lives?" The hypothesis work is not skippable — it's what keeps teams from building things that don't matter.
- **Simulations and audio-over-images as concrete examples**: Two recent launches illustrate her framework — interactive simulators (e.g., "at what angle should I do a basketball shot?" or toggling investment returns) and audio overlaid on images to explain concepts like photosynthesis. Both went from a small "skunk group" with a hypothesis to launch quickly.
- **Quality bar is non-negotiable, even at speed**: She distinguishes between "do it fast" and "do it well" — the instruction is to do both, but quality is the hard floor. A subpar simulation would have fallen flat. Consumers will tell you when the craft isn't there, and they'll stop using the product.
- **SWAT team structure over hierarchies**: She favors lean, flat pods — one senior strategic lead plus a small bench of strong ICs. Multiple management layers kill context and therefore kill speed. She calls this "SWAT team structure" and deliberately keeps communication lines flat.
- **Build horizontally, not vertically**: She encourages being inspired by specific vertical use cases (put yourself in the consumer's journey) but then asks: can this be built horizontally at scale? This breaks organizational silos and avoids building point solutions.
- **AI tooling accelerates both good and bad practices**: She acknowledges that LLMs are great for getting things off the ground — logs analysis, prototyping, brainstorming — but teams must stress-test outputs, know the limits, and keep an honest ongoing conversation about where AI adds value vs. where it doesn't. She says how they build has changed every six months.
- **Two-sided marketplace tension**: Randy raises the content provider problem — Google now quotes and summarizes content rather than driving clickthroughs to publishers. Soufi's response is that links, news integration, and AI-driven fanouts are meant to deepen connections to publishers, not replace them. This tension is real but not fully resolved in the conversation.
- **Overpersonalization is a known risk**: When pushed on why LLM experiences sometimes get worse as they learn more about you, she categorizes this as overpersonalization — making associations that aren't relevant — and says the solution is a combination of traditional quality hill-climbing, thumbs up/down signals, beta tester feedback, and user studies.
- **Three-month horizon as a forcing function**: Regardless of what stage a project is at (frontier exploration or mature product), she asks her team "where do you want to be in three months?" to maintain urgency and clarity without losing sight of longer-term destination.
- **Sustainable org design around areas, not specific solutions**: She structures accountability around durable problem areas (e.g., end-to-end response, consumer framework, long-running journeys) rather than specific technical approaches. Within an area, the method can vary as models and tech evolve. She aims for area longevity of more than 9 months but can't commit to 2–3 years.
- **What she hires for — "Consumer AI PM DNA"**: Three traits: (1) demonstrated ability to build user-facing products and genuinely inhabit the user's perspective; (2) staying current on underlying AI technology and the competitive landscape; (3) zero-to-one / builder / startup mentality — the excitement of making things happen quickly and pushing boundaries.
- **Eliminating noise is a leadership responsibility**: She distinguishes between working urgently (good) and chaotic debate/spinning (bad). Her job as a leader is to spot organizational noise — resource blocks, cross-team debates, duplication — and clear it so the team can do their best work.
- **Speed at scale is a genuine technical differentiator**: Google's expectation of near-instantaneous answers, even for personalized AI-generated responses that can't be pre-cached, is a core engineering challenge that requires dedicated focus separate from product decisions.

## Use cases
- **Senior PMs and product directors** leading AI-era product teams who need a framework for balancing speed with quality
- **PMs at large consumer platforms** figuring out how to disrupt themselves without losing the core value proposition or existing user trust
- **Product leaders doing org design** who are deciding between flat SWAT structures vs. traditional layered teams
- **PMs building personalization features** who need to think through the risks of overpersonalization and how to get feedback signals at scale
- **Hiring managers in product** who are defining what "AI PM" means and what traits to screen for in a new AI-era role
- **Teams drowning in AI-generated ideas** who need a framework for deciding what is actually worth building vs. what is noise
- **PMs at companies with content/publisher relationships** who need to think through both sides of a marketplace when AI changes the value exchange
- **Any PM navigating the prototype-to-production challenge** under pressure of fast-moving model improvements and shifting consumer expectations

## Patterns & frameworks

**Idea → Prototype → Real Data Demo → Beta (fast loop)**
The core process Soufi advocates: don't debate for weeks, don't build decks. Formulate your hypothesis, spin up a prototype quickly, connect it to real data for a scrappy demo showing the bare minimum, and get it in front of beta testers. The goal is to either kill the idea early or generate organizational momentum fast. Explicitly for "non-one-way-door" decisions.

**Two-track product development**
She distinguishes between: (1) the *maintenance track* — continuously improving a live product used by billions through quality hill-climbing, friction analysis, and relentless craft; and (2) the *frontier track* — hypothesis-driven exploration of new ideas that go from zero to beta quickly. Each requires different processes, team energy, and success metrics.

**Hypothesis-first building**
Before any prototype work, the team must answer: "Why does this matter? Why will it resonate? What are the indicators we're helping people?" This is not skippable even when moving fast — it is what separates signal from noise when many things are now technically possible to build.

**SWAT team / flat pod structure**
Lean pods with one senior strategic lead and a small IC bench. No multiple management layers because layers destroy context, context is required to move fast, and fast is required to meet the moment. Communication lines to leadership stay flat specifically to unblock and enable, not to review and approve.

**Horizontal building inspired by vertical use cases**
Start with a specific consumer journey (vertical) to get genuine empathy and a clear definition of "good." Then challenge the team to abstract upward: can we build this capability horizontally so it scales across use cases? Avoids both the trap of over-generalizing too early and the trap of building one-off solutions.

**Area-based org design with flexible methods**
Structure teams around durable problem areas (long-running journeys, end-to-end response, consumer framework), not around current technical approaches or specific model versions. The area stays stable enough for people to build expertise and accountability (target: longer than 9 months); the method of solving problems within that area can and should evolve as the technology changes.

**Three-month forcing function**
Regardless of project stage, always ask: "Where do we want to be in three months?" Creates urgency, prevents drift, and keeps long-horizon vision from becoming an excuse to delay. Works in parallel with longer-term destination clarity — both horizons are active simultaneously.

**Define "good" early and together**
A team-level ritual: before building, show each other what the product looks like when it's good and when it's not. Align on the bar of excellence concretely, not abstractly. Especially important when building new AI features where the quality bar isn't established yet.
