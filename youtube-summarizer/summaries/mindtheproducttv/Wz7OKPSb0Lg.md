# ProductTank Auckland - Who Are We Building For? Product and Sales on ICP and UVP

Video ID: `Wz7OKPSb0Lg`

## Summary
This ProductTank Auckland talk by Ren Taguchi, a 30-year sales veteran turned sales trainer, addresses the persistent tension between product managers and salespeople. The central argument is that both teams are looking at the same elephant from different angles — product protects the roadmap while sales protects revenue targets — and that better collaboration around two shared definitions (ICP and UVP) can resolve most of the conflict. Ren draws on real enterprise deals from companies like Ruckus Wireless and Alcatel-Lucent to make the case concrete. The talk is most relevant to product managers, CPOs, and sales leaders in B2B software or hardware companies navigating roadmap commitments, feature requests, and account prioritization.

## Key insights
- **The core dysfunction:** Product managers protect the roadmap; salespeople protect revenue targets. When a PM shares a roadmap, sales treats it as a committed delivery. When sales forecasts, PMs don't realize it's probabilistic — the same way a roadmap is. Neither side understands the other's operating model.
- **"The map is not the territory"** (Korzybski): Each team's explanation of reality is their own mental model, not objective truth. Empathy — genuinely trying to see through the other person's lens — is the prerequisite for productive collaboration.
- **Derived Economic Value (DEV) is the right unit of measure:** ICP is not defined by your price or features. It's defined by the internal economic value the customer gains — reduced opex, increased ARPU, market share growth. Sales should be able to articulate this transformation, not just list features.
- **Roadmap ≠ commitment:** PMs know their roadmap is directional intent; salespeople universally treat it as a promise to clients. This mismatch is a root cause of broken trust. Ren's recommendation: communicate roadmap items with the same probabilistic language sales uses in forecasting (good/worst/best scenarios).
- **Feature requests must be validated as market signals:** A single customer asking for a feature is not a market signal. Ren's gating model: hold individual requests, watch for recurrence across accounts, and only develop once a recurring pattern emerges — with explicit stages: customer request → recurring market pattern → product opportunity → contractual roadmap.
- **Ruckus Wireless case study:** A global telco customer won a government account and needed security compliance not on the roadmap. Ren had to coordinate BT, Time Warner Cable, Free, and other operators to build a collective business case. It took months to approve and 6–8 months to deliver — but the lesson is that PMs and salespeople working together can turn a "no" into a strategic win rather than a lost deal.
- **Alcatel-Lucent vs. Huawei case study:** Facing a $20M Huawei bid on a $50M deal, Ren's team nearly walked away. The PM found a lower-spec model that met the customer's actual desired outcomes (not the full feature list). By reframing the conversation around outcomes rather than specs, they won the deal — a win impossible without tight product-sales collaboration.
- **The "tiger team" model for fast decisions:** At one telco, a standing three-person team (finance, senior technical, sales head) was empowered to make real-time calls on whether to commit to customer requests. This bypassed slow escalation chains and enabled honest conversations with clients about what could actually be delivered and when.
- **Sales commission plans as a diagnostic tool:** Connor (audience member) and Ren highlighted that PMs should read their sales team's commission structure. It is usually not secret but not advertised. It explains behaviors: if salespeople are only rewarded on revenue, they'll cut price; if profitability is included, they'll protect margin. Knowing the comp plan helps PMs understand motivations and build the relationship.
- **AI and automation shifting PM role:** At the KiwiSaaS conference the same day, speakers noted that the PM role is moving away from design/engineering and toward commercial acumen, pre-sales, and orchestration — making the product-sales relationship even more central going forward.
- **Win-loss reviews are underused:** Most companies don't do them, and salespeople avoid discussing losses. Ren's recommendation: make win-loss reviews a KPI for sales, not optional. The real value is acting on findings — feeding insights back into the roadmap or sales pitch, not just holding a "talk fest."
- **Shared objections log and client language:** Maintaining a running log of objections, lost deal reasons, feature requests, and the actual language customers use is rare but high-value. With AI tools (e.g., Notion), this is now easy to implement and can auto-generate FAQs for the team.

## Use cases
- A PM who keeps getting ambushed by sales escalations instead of being looped in early on account strategy
- A sales leader whose team is over-promising roadmap features to close deals, damaging customer trust at delivery
- A startup that is constantly pivoting and needs a structured way to separate one-off customer requests from genuine market signals
- An enterprise PM at a company where product units sit in different countries/divisions and coordination is slow
- A product team preparing to run quarterly ICP/UVP reviews and needing a framework for what sales should contribute
- A PM who wants to understand why salespeople behave the way they do (read their commission plan)
- Any B2B company competing against low-cost incumbents (e.g., a Huawei equivalent) where winning requires outcome-based positioning rather than feature comparison
- A team responding to RFPs that wants to build a shared, AI-assisted knowledge base to reduce repetitive manual work
- A CPO or sales manager trying to design operating rhythms (joint discovery calls, monthly insight sessions, quarterly reviews) that are structural rather than ad hoc

## Patterns & frameworks

**1. Map is Not the Territory (Korzybski)**
A mental model from Polish-American semanticist Alfred Korzybski: our description of reality is never reality itself. Applied here: when product and sales describe the same situation, they are each sharing their own model, not objective fact. The prescription is empathy — actively trying to understand the other person's model before reacting.

**2. ICP (Ideal Customer Profile) — Sales Definition**
The organization or account that gets the most value from your offering. Defined by: firmographics, technographics (tech stack), compelling events/triggers, problem fit, and commercial fit. Sales layer: who has budget, urgency, decision-making authority, and why deals progress or stall. ICP is described as a *shared commercial decision* — product identifies who uses it and what problems exist; sales validates commercial viability.

**3. UVP (Unique Value Proposition) — Sales Definition**
Not just "what the product does better," but situationally defined: the client's unique situation + their key decision criteria + your solution aligned to their context + evidence that the outcome is achievable. Broken into: client problem and desired results, evidence the problem exists, business impact, and the transformation your product enables.

**4. Derived Economic Value (DEV)**
An internal customer metric: the financial impact your solution creates inside the customer's P&L — opex savings, capex reduction, ARPU increase, market share growth. Replaces feature/price framing. Sales must be able to articulate this to PMs as the "why" behind a deal, and PMs should use it to evaluate whether a feature request is strategically worth building.

**5. The Three Sales Qualification Tests**
Ren's personal framework for prioritizing where to spend time: (1) Who has the biggest, most expensive problem? (2) Do they have to decide *now*? (3) Why are you the best partner to solve it?

**6. Feature Request Gating Model**
A staged process to prevent individual customer demands from hijacking the roadmap:
- Gate 1: Customer request (hold, don't act)
- Gate 2: Recurring market pattern (multiple accounts, same signal)
- Gate 3: Product opportunity (validated commercially)
- Gate 4: Contractual roadmap (committed)

**7. PM-Sales Responsibility Matrix**
A joint framework for dividing work:
| Question | Product leads | Sales contributes |
|---|---|---|
| What problem are we solving? | Defines | Validates commercially |
| Which customers experience it? | Analyzes patterns | Brings market evidence |
| Who will buy/prioritize it? | Contributes | Leads (customer-facing) |
| What should we build? | Decides | Provides evidence |
| What value do we communicate? | Defines product truth | Translates to buyer language |
| What can we promise? | Sets boundaries | Sells within them |
| What do clients ask for repeatedly? | Creates value | Captures value |

**8. Tiger Team**
A small standing escalation committee (finance, senior technical, sales head) empowered to make rapid go/no-go decisions on non-roadmap customer requests. Bypasses slow corporate escalation chains. Enables fast, honest client conversations with a realistic commitment or a clear "no."

**9. Sales Forecasting Analogy for Roadmap Communication**
Roadmaps should be communicated like sales forecasts — with explicit probability tiers (good/worst/best) and regular cadence updates, not as point-in-time commitments. This gives sales the language to set appropriate client expectations rather than over-promising.

**10. Operating Rhythm for Structural Collaboration**
Specific recurring touchpoints Ren recommends: joint client discovery calls, monthly product-sales-customer insight sessions, win-loss reviews (with KPI enforcement), shared objection/request logs, and quarterly ICP/UVP reviews based on actual evidence — not ad hoc escalation-driven conversations.
