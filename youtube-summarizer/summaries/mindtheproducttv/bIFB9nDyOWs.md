# Unpacking Canva’s layoffs: fewer layers, more ownership, less remote work

Video ID: `bIFB9nDyOWs`

## Summary
This episode of *Now Shipping* (hosted by Mike Belceto of Mind the Product) unpacks Canva's announcement that it is laying off roughly 22% of its 5,700-person workforce, using it as a lens to examine a broader wave of tech layoffs in late 2025. Belceto argues that while AI is a contributing factor, slowing growth is the primary driver — and that the structural changes companies are making (fewer layers, broader ownership, more in-person work) reveal what the future product role will actually look like. The video draws on data from Mind the Product's own layoff research, Canva's internal memo, and comparable cuts at Netflix and eBay. It is most relevant to product managers, product leaders, and anyone navigating layoffs — either as a survivor, a person recently let go, or someone trying to stay ahead of the reshaping of the profession.

---

## Key insights
- **Canva's layoff is not primarily AI-driven.** CEO Melanie Perkins explicitly says small changes won't be enough and that growth has slowed for the first time in a decade. Revenue growth forecast was reportedly cut from 30% to 20% for 2026, and valuation was marked down from ~$41.8B to ~$34.9B by investors Blackbird and Airree.
- **Three structural changes Canva is making:** (1) Simplifying org structure by merging product, growth, and platform into one; (2) Reducing sub-specialization and collapsing layers to broaden ownership; (3) Co-locating teams — Sydney becomes the primary hub for product teams, and all other offices require 2–3 days in-person minimum.
- **Canva's moat has eroded.** Canva democratized design for a decade, but AI tools like Claude and ChatGPT now let anyone create with a prompt. Saster.ai founder Jason Lemkin publicly noted his company stopped using Canva a year ago despite continuing to pay — and attributed it to the "new agentic world."
- **AI costs are real and eating margins.** Canva's internal valuation cut was partly tied to rising AI costs from new product rollouts — a warning that shipping AI features without solving pricing and packaging creates a cost-per-user problem when growth slows.
- **Tech product headcount is down 28% from the 2022 peak** (per Livedata Technologies, via Mind the Product research). Managers are down 31%, VP-level product roles down 38%, and senior ICs down 21% — layers are shrinking fastest; individual contributors are holding on longer.
- **Job openings are recovering.** Per Lenny's Newsletter, product job openings are up 75% from the late 2022 low — the jobs aren't disappearing, they're changing shape.
- **AI-motivated layoffs are frequently a cover story.** Research from Pro Cap Insights found that 9 in 10 companies announcing AI-motivated layoffs are underperforming the S&P 500 by ~60 percentage points. Over a third of organizations that replaced workers with AI have already rehired some of them.
- **Netflix cut ~1,000 jobs (~6%)** in October, hitting product, tech, legal, and HR — driven by slowing growth (viewing hours up only 2% in H1) and a stock down 30%+ in six months, not AI.
- **eBay cut 9% of its workforce** in October (second round in 2025 after 800 cuts in February) — framed as simplifying operations and shifting resources to faster-growing parts of the business.
- **Coordination work is what AI is absorbing.** Status updates, first-draft specs, research summaries, meeting-to-Slack recaps — these are the layer tasks that AI handles well. What remains is judgment: what to build, what to cut, when to say no.
- **Fear changes behavior in harmful ways.** People under layoff anxiety default to visibility over impact, stop pushing back on executives, and work on what gets noticed rather than what moves metrics — which ultimately makes cuts more likely, not less.
- **Canva's severance package is notably generous:** 6+ months salary in every country, removal of the 1-year vesting cliff for recent hires, 12 months of healthcare, career coaching, interview prep, 1 year of LinkedIn Premium, an alumni directory shared with investors and partners, laptops kept, and a $10,000 no-equity startup grant.
- **The $10,000 startup grant is a signal.** Belceto highlights this as especially interesting — combined with AI tools that let a product person build a working prototype in a weekend (or hours), the barrier to building something new has collapsed. The bottleneck is now judgment about what's worth building, not capital or engineering resources.

## Use cases
- **Active product managers** who want to future-proof their role by owning outcomes end-to-end rather than narrow feature slices.
- **Product leaders managing nervous teams** after a layoff announcement — specifically how to communicate honestly without false reassurance or vague platitudes.
- **Laid-off product professionals** figuring out what to do next: how to leave with the right things, document wins while fresh, network before feeling ready, and evaluate IC vs. leadership roles.
- **PMs assessing their product's moat** — the "prompt test" exercise is directly applicable to any product team worried about AI substitution.
- **Anyone shipping AI features** who needs to think through pricing and packaging before rising cost-per-user erodes margins.
- **Aspiring founders** (especially those with product backgrounds) considering whether a layoff moment is the right time to build something new.
- **Org designers and CPOs** evaluating whether their team structure has too many layers, too much sub-specialization, or too much distributed ownership — and what a "refounding" might look like.

## Patterns & frameworks

**The "Refound" Framework (Canva's restructuring logic)**
Perkins uses the word "refound" deliberately — returning to startup-era small empowered teams with end-to-end ownership. The pattern: collapse sub-specializations into broader roles → reduce coordination layers → co-locate closely connected teams → give teams ownership of outcomes from first idea to metrics dashboard. Applicable when a scaling company has grown too fragmented to move fast.

**The Prompt Moat Test**
Take the core job your product does and write the prompt a user might use to get the same thing done. Run it in Claude, ChatGPT, Grok, or all three. If what comes back is 80% as good as your product, that's a signal your moat has eroded and you need to rethink differentiation. Simple, repeatable, and can be done in under an hour.

**The One-Outcome Job Description Exercise**
Write down one outcome for your product that you want to fully own. Articulate it in one or two sentences clear enough to serve as a job description. If you can't do that, the ambiguity is the problem to fix — because when your company writes its own restructuring memo pointing to end-to-end ownership, your sentence is what keeps you in the room.

**AI Cost vs. Growth Equation**
A mental model for AI feature decisions: if growth is slowing but cost-per-user is rising (because AI inference isn't free), you have a pricing and packaging problem, not just a product one. Forces the question: what's included in the base tier, what's metered, and what's worth a premium?

**The "Visible vs. Impact" Fear Trap**
Under layoff pressure, people optimize for visibility rather than impact — doing what gets noticed rather than what moves metrics. Belceto (via Jenny Wanger) names this as a self-reinforcing trap: the visible work doesn't prevent cuts, it just delays accountability. The counter-pattern is to stay anchored to measurable outcomes even when the environment rewards signaling.

**Layoff Communication Framework (for managers)**
Three rules: (1) Avoid false reassurance — "everything's fine" destroys trust the moment it isn't; (2) Be specific — replace vague long-term language with what the company is prioritizing this quarter and what it means for their work; (3) Commit to transparency — tell the team you'll tell them the moment you know more. Silence forces people to fill gaps themselves, and they stop taking risks when they do.
