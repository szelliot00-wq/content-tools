# How to hire and develop juniors in an AI-first product org

Video ID: `E92tSzhMqkw`

## Summary
This episode of the Product Experience podcast features Tiana, Chief Product and Technology Officer at Opus 2 (a legal tech company), discussing how AI is reshaping product careers, team structure, and how organizations should develop junior talent. The core argument is that AI accelerates output but does not replace the need for human judgment, curiosity, and outcome-orientation — and that the quality markers of good product work (clarity of strategy, customer focus, measurable value) remain unchanged. The conversation is most relevant to product leaders, hiring managers, and early-career product people navigating an AI-first workplace.

## Key insights
- **Burnout is a real side effect of AI adoption.** Tiana observes that many teams feel overloaded rather than relieved by AI, because the expectation shifts to constant output maximization. She actively pushes back on this by telling her teams to go outside, take breaks, and protect time for human-only thinking.
- **"Effectiveness maxing" vs. token maxing.** Tiana distinguishes between raw output volume (PRs getting larger, potentially full of AI slop) and actual effectiveness. Her team uses DX scores and DORA metrics to verify that AI adoption is improving quality, not just quantity.
- **Large PR sizes are a warning sign.** She uses bloated pull request sizes as a proxy indicator that AI-generated code ("slop") may be going in without sufficient human review.
- **Outcome-orientation over craft attachment is the #1 filter.** She looks for team members who care about the result, not the specific seven-step process they used to use. People who self-identify as preferring the old craft are encouraged to find companies where that fits — not a criticism, just a mismatch.
- **Curiosity is the second key trait.** The best performers in an AI-first org are energized by not knowing things — whether they're new to their career or 20-year veterans encountering unfamiliar territory for the first time in decades.
- **A sharing culture is the third pillar.** Because no one has figured out AI adoption fully, openly sharing what works and what fails — across seniority levels — is essential. Tiana plans to share her own AI operating system with her whole team, including how she does board reporting and competitive briefing.
- **Agentic projects will fail if not tied to strategy.** She cites a Gartner-level projection that 40% of corporate agentic projects will fail, and attributes this to companies "just doing AI" without strategic grounding. She connects this to patterns she sees in M&A diligence.
- **77% of jobs are still cheaper with humans** (citing an industry figure), which informs her decision to keep humans in the loop at Opus 2 rather than automating them out.
- **AI expenditure budgets are up ~300–320%**, driven by agentic systems consuming more usage — though she bets costs will come down over time as supply/demand normalize.
- **Opus 2's AI strategy is human-augmentation, not replacement.** Their nine-agent hearings assist system in UK courtrooms is designed to reduce mental load on staff and expand capacity, not eliminate roles. Customer trust in the human element is a key reason.
- **Documentation and planning become MORE critical with AI, not less.** Because AI compresses build cycles, the natural pauses where teams used to align and course-correct disappear. Explicit outcome documentation and architectural decision boundaries must fill that gap.
- **Junior hires are a diversity and perspective strategy.** Tiana continues hiring juniors deliberately — not just for growth, but because they bring fresh perspectives and may be less invested in defending old ways of working. She notes seniors sometimes have "more to lose" psychologically.
- **AI slop is detectable, and 30/60/90-day onboarding still applies.** She holds juniors to the same quality expectations as seniors, and relies on managers being more diligent at spotting LLM-generated vs. genuine thinking in deliverables.
- **CV advice is the one exception to "write the first 50% yourself."** Because CVs pass through opaque algorithmic screening, she accepts using an LLM to generate a first draft optimized for keywords — but recommends rewriting it in your own voice afterward to avoid sounding artificial to human reviewers.
- **Opus 2 moved from shipping every 6–12 weeks to shipping daily.** This wasn't purely AI-driven; it was paired with dropping PI planning and other process overhead, giving teams more agency.
- **"Add lightness" as a product philosophy.** She references a Lotus designer's principle — if you add something, add lightness — as her mental model for resisting feature bloat, especially in high-stakes legal software.
- **Sales to product is an underrated path.** Tiana's background in sales (driven by listening to customer problems and building scalable solutions) maps naturally onto product thinking, and she challenges the assumption that engineering background is a prerequisite.

## Use cases
- **Product leaders scaling teams** who need a framework for deciding when to hire vs. when AI can absorb capacity.
- **CPOs/CTOs evaluating AI adoption maturity** using leading indicators like PR size, DX scores, and DORA metrics rather than just adoption rates.
- **Managers onboarding junior PMs or engineers** in an AI-first environment, who need to distinguish genuine thinking from AI-generated output.
- **Junior product people and engineers** figuring out how to build real skills and judgment when AI can do much of the mechanical work for them.
- **Hiring managers** redesigning scorecards and CV screening processes that have been distorted by LLM-generated applications.
- **Executive teams debating agentic AI projects**, who need a strategic (not FOMO-driven) framework for deciding what to build.
- **Product organizations in regulated or high-stakes industries** (legal, healthcare, finance) where "ship because we can" is not acceptable and human trust is a differentiator.
- **Anyone experiencing AI-related burnout** who needs permission and framing to protect cognitive recovery time.

## Patterns & frameworks

**Outcome-over-craft filter**
When evaluating team members' AI readiness, Tiana looks for whether someone cares about the result vs. the process. Practically: ask whether someone can drop their seven-step process and instead ask "what outcome do I need, and how do I partner with AI to reach it?" People who can't decouple process from outcome are flagged as a culture mismatch, not a deficiency.

**Three pillars of AI-ready team culture**
1. Outcome orientation (not craft attachment)
2. Curiosity (excited by uncertainty, not threatened by it)
3. Sharing culture (positive and negative learnings shared openly across levels)

**"Write the first 50%" rule**
For internal work, Tiana requires team members to author the first half of any deliverable themselves before involving AI. This preserves the ability to think strategically, form sentences, and maintain a genuine voice. The exception is CV writing, where algorithmic screening justifies leading with AI.

**AI adoption as outcome reinvention, not step substitution**
Rather than mapping AI onto an existing workflow ("I had 7 steps, AI does 3"), the model Tiana advocates is: define the desired outcome, partner with AI to reach it fresh, then layer in human judgment at the decision points — which may not have been explicit steps before but were implicit judgment calls.

**DX + DORA as dual-axis AI adoption scorecard**
Adoption metrics alone (time saved, AI usage rate) are insufficient. Tiana pairs them with quality metrics (PR size as a slop proxy, DORA scores for delivery health, DX snapshot scores for team experience) to verify that speed gains aren't degrading output quality.

**Humans-in-the-loop as a strategic customer retention bet**
Rather than treating human roles as a cost to optimize away, Opus 2 frames human involvement as a product feature that customers actively prefer — especially in high-stakes contexts. Agentic systems are designed to expand what those humans can do, not replace them.

**30/60/90 onboarding with an explicit "value in year one" document**
Applied consistently regardless of seniority level, this structure gives managers a baseline against which to evaluate genuine thinking vs. AI-generated output, and gives new hires a clear definition of what success looks like.

**"Add lightness" as a feature-shipping principle**
Borrowed from automotive design, this mental model asks: does adding this feature make the product lighter and simpler for the user? If not, the default should be not to ship it — particularly relevant when AI makes building trivially easy and the bottleneck shifts to judgment about what to build.
