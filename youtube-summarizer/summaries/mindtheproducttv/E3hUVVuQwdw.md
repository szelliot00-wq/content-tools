# What your developers aren't telling you — Dr Cat Hicks (Psychologist and Founder)

Video ID: `E3hUVVuQwdw`

## Summary
Dr. Cat Hicks, a psychologist and founder of the consultancy Catharsis, discusses the psychological dynamics of software teams based on years of open-access research she conducted through the Developer Success Lab at Pluralsight. Her core argument is that the dominant "brains and jars" mental model — treating developers as interchangeable, isolated cognitive units — causes measurable harm to productivity, culture, and individual wellbeing. She presents research-backed alternatives that reframe developers as whole humans embedded in social environments, and argues that most performance problems are systemic rather than individual. The conversation is most relevant to product managers, engineering leaders, and anyone who manages or collaborates with technical teams.

## Key insights
- **The "brains and jars" model is pervasive and damaging.** Leaders and even developers themselves often unconsciously view software development as a purely individual, cognitive, interchangeable activity — like widgets on a factory line. This model suppresses collaboration, mentorship, psychological safety, and learning behaviors.
- **Developer productivity is highly variable over time, not a stable individual trait.** A study across 216 companies and 11,000+ developers showed that the same individual looks fast some weeks and slow others. This means sorting people into "fast" and "slow" developers is misleading and wastes organizational resources.
- **Most performance friction lives in the environment, not the individual.** When work stalls, the cause is usually environmental friction or problem complexity, not individual capability. Leaders should diagnose and reduce systemic blockers rather than attributing slowness to people.
- **Over-production pressure cascades into bad team behavior.** When developers consistently feel short-term output goals always beat long-term quality goals, they self-censor, stop mentoring juniors, hide problems from managers, and disengage from upskilling — all of which compound over time.
- **Code review anxiety is real and underreported.** Highly experienced developers participated in Dr. Hicks's code review anxiety study specifically because they had never told anyone about their paralysing anxiety before — suggesting this is widespread and invisible to leadership.
- **AI is creating identity threats, not just workflow changes.** Developers feel visceral fear about skill devaluation ("AI skill threats"). Teams that offer safe off-ramps — messaging like "we'll help you learn new skills, technology always changes, we face it together" — measurably lower threat response and enable people to engage constructively with AI.
- **AI use is more learning-oriented than it appears.** Even developers who express anxiety about AI describe use cases that are fundamentally about learning: exploring old codebases, understanding unfamiliar languages. Dr. Hicks sees this as grounds for optimism.
- **Agentic coding is creating a new kind of cognitive exhaustion.** Constantly context-switching to approve agent outputs, making rapid hard decisions all morning, is a distinct burnout pathway. Teams need to build in larger cycles of deep focus and reflection, not just faster approval loops.
- **Metacognitive scaffolding is underused in software teams.** Beyond raw cognitive capacity, people can be trained in strategies for thinking (deliberate practice, structured feedback loops). A study of 40,000 chess players showed dramatically faster skill improvement with deliberate practice. Dr. Hicks argues this design space is largely unbuilt for developer tooling.
- **Dr. Hicks built a Claude skill called "learning opportunities"** that quizzes developers on agent-generated code after the fact, prompting them to articulate how things work. Thousands of developers adopted it, demonstrating demand for metacognitive tools embedded in agentic workflows.
- **Caring deeply about your work is a burnout risk, not just a virtue.** The expectation that everyone must be emotionally fuelled by the mission is exhausting. Teams with diversity of motivation — including people who are solid contributors without being deeply passionate — are healthier.
- **Superordinate goals are the practical bridge across collaboration styles.** Finding the larger shared goal (e.g. users achieving an outcome) that transcends individual working styles allows diverse team members — including those who prefer to be left alone — to collaborate without requiring uniformity of approach or personality.
- **Culture change on over-production pressure can happen in weeks, not years.** When a leader explicitly supports a developer who chose a long-term goal over a short-term one, that single act can begin shifting team beliefs about what is actually safe and valued.
- **The social side of technical work is required but unrewarded.** Organizations say they need collaboration and mentorship, then never promote for it or celebrate it publicly — a contradiction that erodes trust.
- **Psychological safety and genuine listening compound.** Developers who feel unheard lose practice articulating their thinking in cross-functional settings, which makes them feel worse, which makes them withdraw further. Breaking that cycle with curiosity and a listening ear has outsized returns.

## Use cases
- **Product managers** looking to improve relationships with engineering teams and understand what developers actually need from them.
- **Engineering leaders** trying to move away from story-point or lines-of-code metrics toward measures that reflect real value and systemic health.
- **Team leads** noticing burnout, disengagement, or communication breakdowns who want research-backed frameworks for diagnosing root causes.
- **HR and people teams** designing performance review systems, onboarding, or learning programs for technical staff.
- **Organizations adopting agentic AI coding tools** who want to avoid the cognitive exhaustion and skill-decay risks associated with poorly structured AI workflows.
- **CTOs or VPs of Engineering** trying to justify engineering investment to business stakeholders using better metrics than velocity.
- **Anyone managing knowledge workers** (journalists, creatives, marketers) — Dr. Hicks notes the core psychology transfers broadly beyond software.
- **Developers themselves** experiencing anxiety, identity threat from AI, or feeling undervalued — the research validates these experiences and offers reframes.
- **Researchers or toolbuilders** looking for unbuilt design space: metacognitive and learning scaffolding embedded in developer tooling is identified as a wide-open opportunity.

## Patterns & frameworks

**Brains and Jars Model**
The dominant (flawed) mental model of software development: developers are isolated, interchangeable cognitive units producing output like a factory. Named after the evil-scientist-lab image of brains in jars. Causes teams to undervalue collaboration, mentorship, learning, and social work. Recognized immediately by tech audiences because it matches lived experience.

**Over-Production Pressure (survey measure)**
A validated survey instrument Dr. Hicks wrote that measures how often developers feel that short-term output goals always beat long-term quality goals. Acts as a leading indicator: high scores correlate with self-censorship, reduced mentoring, hiding problems from managers, and reduced upskilling. Can be introduced into any organization; Dr. Hicks offers to share it on request.

**AI Skill Threats**
A framework from Dr. Hicks's early (2023–2024) AI research. Describes the visceral identity threat developers feel when they imagine that hard-won, valued skills will no longer earn them a place in technical work. Distinct from general job anxiety — it is about skill identity. Teams reduce this by providing explicit "safe off-ramps": messaging that normalizes skill transition and collective resilience.

**Metacognitive Scaffolding**
Borrowed from psychology and learning science. Distinguishes between raw cognitive capacity (memory, processing speed) and learnable strategies for thinking (deliberate practice, structured reflection, mental model articulation). Metacognitive strategies are malleable — they can be trained. Applied to agentic coding: tools that prompt developers to explain and quiz themselves on generated code are metacognitive interventions, not just productivity features.

**Superordinate Goals**
A concept from social psychology. These are shared higher-order goals that transcend individual working styles, preferences, or sub-goals. Used to bridge collaboration between developers who want to be left alone and those who want to engage broadly: you don't need everyone to work the same way, but you do need enough of a shared superordinate goal to make collaboration functional. Identifying these goals is a practical leadership move.

**Deliberate Practice (referenced)**
Structured, feedback-rich practice as a metacognitive strategy. Cited via a study of 40,000 chess players showing significantly faster skill development with deliberate practice vs. unstructured practice. Presented as a model for how developer learning should be designed — not just more hours, but better-structured feedback at the right time.

**Learning Opportunities Claude Skill**
A concrete tool Dr. Hicks built: a Claude skill that, after an agentic coding session, identifies teachable moments in the generated code relevant to the developer's codebase and quizzes them on it. Adopted by several thousand developers. Illustrates how metacognitive scaffolding can be embedded directly into agentic workflows rather than treated as separate training.
