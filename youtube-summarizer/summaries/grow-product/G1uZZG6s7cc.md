# How to Steal Your Way to a Hit Product (Zynga Founder's Whiteboard)

Video ID: `G1uZZG6s7cc`

## Summary
Mark Pincus, founder of Zynga, presents his "Proven Better New" product development framework on this podcast episode with Akos Stádler. The core argument is that copying successful products is not only acceptable but strategically essential — the best product builders copy proven experiences pixel-by-pixel, then add targeted improvements and a narrow slice of genuine innovation. Pincus draws on examples from Zynga Poker, Farmville, Supercell's Heyday, and current AI consumer apps to illustrate when and how much "new" to pursue depending on where you are in a platform cycle. The episode is most relevant to founders, PMs, and product builders deciding what to build next, especially in the current early AI cycle.

---

## Key insights

- **Copying is a skill, not a shortcut.** Pincus argues all products are derivative — the question is who executes the "magic trick" better. Steve Jobs was great at it; Bill Gates less so; Zuckerberg visibly worse. The better product builder you are, the better you copy.
- **Proven = pixel-level copying.** "Proven" doesn't mean the general concept — it means replicating click-by-click, sound-by-sound, color-by-color what has already worked for your target audience on your target platform. Zynga Poker's table was indistinguishable from competitor poker tables by design.
- **Better = what 10/10 existing users would say "f*** yes" to.** Not what *you* think is better — that's actually "New" in disguise. Better is micro-level polish: no download, faster load, shorter crop cycles, touch optimization. If even one user wouldn't want it, it's probably New, not Better.
- **New = your innovation zone, and all new fails.** New is your hypothesis. Pincus's mantra inside Zynga: "All new fails." Start with that expectation. New is sacred when it works, so don't over-invest in it emotionally. Zynga Poker's "New" was real identities via the Facebook social graph — and it worked 25% of the time (friends at the same table), but the product would have survived even if it hadn't.
- **Platform cycle determines how much New to pursue.** Early in a platform shift (internet, mobile, AI now), use almost zero New — the platform IS the new thing. Late in a cycle, you need a lot of New to find a vein. Supercell's Heyday was essentially 0% New — a near-identical Farmville built for mobile — and it was the right call at the start of mobile.
- **The Zynga Poker canonical example:**
  - *Proven:* Copied leading poker games pixel-for-pixel (table layout, chip sounds, chip colors)
  - *Better:* No download (80–90% drop-off eliminated at top of funnel; casual players don't need payment security)
  - *New:* Real identities via Facebook/MySpace social graph; playing with actual friends
- **The Heyday/Farmville example:** Supercell copied Farmville's half-circle driveway, white mailbox, blue pickup truck. Better: built natively for mobile (touch, faster load, shorter crop cycles). New: essentially none — correct for early mobile cycle.
- **Failing for the wrong reasons is the real enemy.** If your onboarding is broken, you never prove your "New" hypothesis. Sid Meier's social Civilization and EA's Social SimCity both died because their game-designer instinct for long tutorials clashed with casual Facebook game requirements (good stuff in 3 clicks, not 3 minutes).
- **The Retro app failure example.** Instagram alumni built Retro to recapture early Instagram simplicity. Pincus says they applied Proven wrong — they cited Instagram as "proven," but today's Instagram looks nothing like Retro, making it actually 15-year-old proven. Also, their onboarding was too many clicks: dead on arrival.
- **Copy everything by default, cut only with a specific user reason.** Farmtown's marketplace (stranger-to-stranger trading) was cut from Farmville because it conflicted with the core user (middle-aged women on Facebook who didn't want stranger danger). Everything else was copied. The neighbor bar — which looked like added friction — was copied and turned out to be a virality engine (two-way opt-in reminders, social density).
- **Instinct/intrinsic target user matters enormously.** Building for an audience you are part of is faster and more accurate. You can feel when something is wrong rather than guessing in the dark.
- **Right now (AI cycle), resist being too clever.** Pincus told Harvard MBA students and Union Square Ventures founders they were being too clever. A perfume-for-sleep startup: too niche for this moment. FOMO (social crypto trading app, $500M ARR) and Paraform (recruiter marketplace with AI leverage, $150M ARR) succeeded by being Better, not radically New.
- **Testing 100 ideas > building one MVP.** The word "viable" in MVP creates false hope. Instead, test top-of-funnel assumptions first — with ads, landing pages, AI agents — before committing to build. Pocket FM's benchmark: 2.25% click-through rate = path to $10M ARR in 3 months in any country. That kind of signal is achievable pre-build.
- **Consumer tech is more investable now than in June 2025.** Most of America (Pincus's Chicago family as a proxy) is just discovering AI. The VC floodgates aren't open yet — that's the opportunity window, not a warning sign. AI-native versions of every app on your phone don't exist yet.
- **Think Weekend.** In March 2006, Pincus hosted ~35 people (including Zuckerberg, Parker, Thiel, Reed Hoffman) at his Aspen ranch. No agenda, no networking, no small talk — only deep intellectual conversation on big topics. The value came from zero transactional intent. The lesson: you can replicate this at any network scale, not just with legends.

## Use cases

- **Founders deciding what product to build next** — use Proven Better New to scope how much copying vs. innovation is appropriate given the platform cycle stage.
- **PMs building v1 of a new product** — use it to avoid failing for the wrong reasons (bad onboarding, wrong defaults) before your core hypothesis is ever tested.
- **Product teams evaluating a competitor** — deconstruct competitor products into Proven/Better/New to decide exactly what to copy, what to improve, and what to replace.
- **Investors or founders evaluating consumer AI** — use the framework to assess whether a startup is being appropriately simple (Better-heavy) for the current early-AI moment.
- **Growth teams testing new ideas** — use top-of-funnel ad/landing page tests (not full builds) to validate click-through rates before committing engineering resources.
- **Anyone entering a new platform** — calibrate how much "New" is appropriate: less at the start of a new platform cycle, more when a market matures.
- **Entrepreneurs building in non-tech categories** (e.g., CPG, hot sauce) — the Proven Better New structure still applies: copy the best-proven taste/formula, identify a clear Better, then find a narrow New.
- **People running small intellectual gatherings** — the Think Weekend model applies at any scale: unconference format, no agenda, no networking, deep topics, catalytic presentations.

## Patterns & frameworks

### Proven Better New
The central framework of the episode. Three zones that together define a new product:

| Zone | Definition | How to apply |
|---|---|---|
| **Proven** | Pixel-level copy of what has already worked for your audience on your platform | Copy it legally and completely — changing things here is the mistake |
| **Better** | Improvements that 10/10 existing users of the proven product would say "f*** yes" to | Must be universal agreement; if some users wouldn't want it, it's probably New |
| **New** | Your novel hypothesis — the innovation zone | Isolate it tightly; assume it will fail; treat it as a testable variable, not the product's identity |

**Key rule:** The amount of New appropriate is inversely proportional to how early you are in a platform cycle. Early = near-zero New. Late = lots of New required.

---

### Platform Cycle Calibration
A meta-framework layered on top of Proven Better New:
- **Early cycle** (e.g., early mobile, early AI now): The platform itself is the New thing. Be mostly Proven + Better. Heyday was the right example.
- **Late cycle** (e.g., mobile today, non-AI): Market is saturated. You need significant New to find a vein.
- **Implication for AI now:** Don't build AI-powered perfume-for-sleep startups. Build AI-enabled versions of things people already do.

---

### All New Fails (mental model)
Treat every New as a hypothesis that will probably fail. Benefits:
1. You won't over-invest emotionally.
2. You won't stay too long with a failing idea.
3. When New does work, you treat it with appropriate reverence and attention.

---

### Top-of-Funnel-First Testing (vs. MVP)
Instead of building a full MVP (which creates false hope via the word "viable"), test assumptions at the top of the funnel first — through ads, landing pages, or AI agents running experiments. Only build once you have a signal (e.g., Pocket FM's 2.25% CTR benchmark for $10M ARR viability).

---

### Trust Your Instinct + Assume Your Idea Is Wrong
Two complementary operating rules:
- **Instinct is right ~95% of the time** (pattern recognition about spaces, vibes, timing)
- **Your specific idea is right ~25% of the time** (the execution, the feature set, the exact form)
- Implication: trust the direction, stay skeptical about the implementation.

---

### Think Weekend (culture pattern)
A format for high-quality intellectual conversation:
- Small group (~35 people), curated for depth not status
- No agenda, no networking, no transactional goals
- Presentations are catalysts for debate, not performances
- Unconference structure — attendees drive direction
- The value comes entirely from zero transactional intent
