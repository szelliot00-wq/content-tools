# Why 99.6% of You Will Never Learn Product Strategy

Video ID: `u2NFqrbvAeY`

## Summary
Tim Herbig, a product strategy expert and author of *Real Progress*, argues that the AI era's ability to produce features cheaply has made *not choosing the right feature* more expensive than ever — making product strategy more critical, not less. The core thesis is that a strategy only counts if it enables teams to say no to things, and that the fundamental questions strategy must answer (narrative, playing field, winning moves) haven't changed despite AI. Herbig walks through a fictional used car marketplace (Fair Mile) to make abstract strategy concepts concrete. The video is most relevant to product managers, heads of product, and CPOs who feel overwhelmed by AI-driven output pressure and want a rigorous, practical approach to strategy that holds up under scrutiny.

## Key insights

- **Cheap features make wrong features more expensive.** When AI dramatically lowers the cost to ship, the cost of shipping the *wrong thing* rises proportionally — you flood the product with output but still have to make customers adopt it.
- **A strategy is only a strategy if it enables a "no."** If your strategy document can't be used to reject an opportunity, it isn't a strategy — it's a wish list. This is the single most important test.
- **Difficulty prioritizing is a strategy problem, not an execution problem.** (Quoting Ravi Mehta.) Execution was never the bottleneck; unclear strategic choices were.
- **AI is a resource, not a value proposition.** No technology in history has been a value proposition on its own. AI amplifies existing reasons customers come to you; it doesn't create new ones. Everyone has access to the same frontier models, so you cannot differentiate on AI alone.
- **The only sustainable differentiators are the ones that take the longest to build.** Brand and data/network effects are the two remaining true moats. You can destroy brand quickly but cannot build it overnight. Process power and captured AI resources are easily copied.
- **Roger Martin's "stupidity flip" test.** A strategic choice is real only if the *opposite* of that choice is not obviously stupid. "Make salespeople more efficient" fails — the opposite (make them less efficient) is obviously stupid, meaning no real choice was made. "Make salespeople more efficient by shortening meeting preparation time" passes — the opposite (lengthen preparation time) is a real alternative someone could choose.
- **Strategy creation is nonlinear and messy.** Canvases and frameworks (business model canvas, lean canvas, strategy kernel) are windows into strategy work, not the strategy itself. Don't start with the template; start with the core questions.
- **The four core questions every product strategy must answer:**
  1. Strategic narrative — why us, why now?
  2. Playing field — for whom, solving what problem, against which alternatives?
  3. Winning moves — how do we win, what value do we provide, how do we differentiate?
  4. Metrics — how will we know the strategy worked?
- **Audience trees create focused segments.** Borrow from Andy Johns: your target audience must be "comically narrow." Slice by region, income, car value, first-time vs. repeat buyer, etc. Land on something specific enough to say no to other segments — e.g., "repeat buyers of cars worth more than €100K in Germany."
- **Segment choice has downstream consequences.** Once your audience is dialed in, the problem space narrows automatically — you can only solve problems for the audience you've chosen. In a marketplace, locking in the buyer segment simultaneously clarifies the seller segment.
- **Hamilton Helmer's Seven Powers as differentiator lens.** Of the seven (switching costs, process power, counterpositioning, brand, network effects, economies of scale, captured resource), only brand and network effects are truly hard to copy in the AI era. For Fair Mile specifically: brand + network effects (buyer/seller lock-in) + dealership economies of scale are the viable bets; AI search is not a sustainable differentiator.
- **Avoid machine-centric metrics.** Tokens used, LLM efficiency, and AI output volume are value exchanges for Anthropic, not for your customers. Strategy success metrics must measure *whose behavior changed, by how much*.
- **The strategy-metrics loop.** Start in plain English: "What will we do differently?" Then: "How will we know it worked?" Only then translate into OKRs or dashboards. Generic KPIs (revenue, inventory) have too much noise and lag to tell you whether *this* strategy choice worked. Use metrics specifically tied to each strategy choice — e.g., "recommendation score >8 among German buyers of cars >€100K."
- **The product strategy stack (Ravi Mehta).** Strategy cascades: company mission → company strategy → product strategy → roadmap → goals. Each level should narrow the funnel. At each level, ask: can I inherit this choice from above, or do I need to break it down further? The test remains the same — does it let me say no?
- **Evergreen benefits principle (from Personio).** Rather than reinventing strategy at the product level, ask: what is the company already incredibly good at, and how does my product strategy leverage it? Distribution channels, existing integrations, and tech capabilities shouldn't be re-derived at every level.
- **Roadmap items must map back to strategy metrics.** Every item on your roadmap should be traceable to the specific metrics that resulted from your strategy choices. If you can't draw that line, the work probably doesn't belong on the roadmap.

## Use cases

- **A PM tasked with defining strategy for the next half:** Use the stupidity flip to audit your existing strategy choices; use audience trees to achieve segment focus; check that your metrics tie directly to those choices rather than generic KPIs.
- **A head of product inheriting a company strategy:** Apply the product strategy stack — identify what can be inherited as-is vs. what needs to be broken down one level further for your product area.
- **A company excited about AI-accelerated shipping:** Ask whether increased output has translated into customer behavior change. If feature adoption is suffering, it's a strategy problem, not an execution problem.
- **A PM being asked to "put AI in the product":** Pressure-test whether AI is being proposed as a value proposition (it isn't one) or as a resource that amplifies an existing reason customers choose your product.
- **A product team drowning in competing priorities:** Use the "does it help us say no?" test against your current strategy document. If you can't say no to anything using it, you don't have a strategy yet.
- **A marketplace product (two-sided):** Use audience trees on both sides simultaneously — your buyer segment definition constrains your seller segment, giving coherence to two separate teams' strategies.
- **An individual IC PM nested under a larger product organization:** Ask what choices have already been made at the level above and which ones you still need to make opinionated choices about for your specific area.
- **A company building differentiation against well-funded AI competitors:** Focus investment on brand and network effects — the only differentiators that are genuinely hard to copy — rather than trying to out-AI competitors on model capabilities.

## Patterns & frameworks

**1. The Stupidity Flip (Roger Martin / Tim Herbig)**
Test every strategic choice by asking: is the opposite of this choice obviously stupid? If yes, you haven't made a real choice — anything could satisfy the strategy. If the opposite is a legitimate (if suboptimal) alternative, you've made a real choice. Apply this to individual *components* of strategy (audience, value prop, differentiation), not just the whole strategy statement. Goal: enforce the "no."

**2. The Four Core Strategy Questions**
Every product strategy must answer: (1) Narrative — why us, why now? (2) Playing Field — for whom, solving what problem, vs. which alternatives? (3) Winning Moves — how do we win and differentiate? (4) Metrics — how will we know it worked? These questions don't change; only the tools used to answer them do. Frameworks like the business model canvas are just "windows" into these four questions.

**3. Audience Trees**
Break your broadest possible audience down by slicing dimensions (region, income, car value, buyer experience level, etc.). Aim between "all potential buyers in Europe" (too broad) and "first-time buyers of vintage Mercedes in southern Germany" (comically narrow). Use AI to rapidly size each node. Land on a segment specific enough to say no to others — e.g., "repeat buyers of cars >€100K in Germany." Locking the audience narrows the problem space automatically.

**4. Hamilton Helmer's Seven Powers (applied to AI era)**
The seven sources of durable competitive advantage: switching costs, process power, counterpositioning, brand, network effects, economies of scale, captured resource. In the AI era, only brand and network effects remain truly hard to replicate. Process power can be caught up to; captured AI resources (access to frontier models) are available to everyone. Evaluate your winning moves against this lens to avoid betting on non-durable differentiators.

**5. The Product Strategy Stack (Ravi Mehta)**
A cascading funnel: company mission → company strategy → product strategy → roadmap → goals. Each level inherits constraints from above and must narrow the funnel further. At each level, ask: (a) what can I inherit as-is? (b) what choices are still undecided and need to be made opinionated here? The same test applies at every level: does this choice enable a "no"?

**6. The Strategy-Metrics Loop**
After forming a strategy, derive metrics by asking in plain English: "What will we do differently?" and "How will we know it worked?" Metrics must be tied to specific strategy choices (e.g., "share of cars sold >€100K" tied to the high-end segment choice), not generic KPIs (revenue, inventory) that can move for unrelated reasons. Map every roadmap item back to these metrics to maintain the narrative link between execution and strategy.

**7. Evergreen Benefits (Personio concept)**
Before defining product strategy, identify what the company is already exceptionally good at (a distribution channel, an integration, a brand reputation, a data asset). Build your product strategy to leverage those existing strengths rather than creating new capabilities from scratch. Ensures coherence across the strategy stack.
