# How to Spot Real Passion in Interviews

Video ID: `kETmsuG_TR4`

## Summary
This video teaches interviewers how to distinguish genuine passion from performed passion in senior PM candidates. The core argument is that experienced PMs are skilled at giving compelling, rehearsed answers to direct questions about motivation, making those answers unreliable signals. The real signal is in the *questions a candidate chooses to ask*, which reveal their true priorities without the filter of knowing what the interviewer wants to hear. The advice is most relevant to hiring managers evaluating director-level or GPM-level product managers.

## Key insights
- Sophisticated PMs are experts at persuasion and inspiration — that's their job. So when you ask "What makes you interested in this role?", you will almost always get an impressive, passionate-sounding answer, regardless of their actual depth of interest.
- The standard interview question "What makes you interested in this role?" is a poor test for passion at senior levels precisely because senior candidates know exactly what answer you're looking for and can deliver it fluently.
- The questions a candidate asks you are a far more reliable signal of passion than their answers to your questions, because candidates are less guarded and less coached about what they reveal through their own curiosity.
- **Candidate A** (lower passion signal) asked: (1) What's the size and growth plan of the team? (2) Who are the cross-functional leaders I'll work with? (3) What are the growth prospects / 6- and 12-month expectations for this role? These are reasonable questions, but they are fundamentally about the candidate's own career trajectory and working conditions.
- **Candidate B** (higher passion signal) asked: (1) Where is the product most falling short in meeting customer needs? (2) The product isn't addressing use cases A, B, and C — is that an intentional strategic choice or something being worked on? (3) Does the team have clarity on product strategy and prioritization for the next 1–2 years? These questions demonstrate prior research and genuine curiosity about the product and customers.
- Candidate A's questions, while not bad, reveal that their primary lens is "what does this role mean for me?" Candidate B's questions reveal that their primary lens is "what is the real state of this product and its customers?"
- It is easy to be impressed by Candidate A in the moment, which is exactly the trap. The video explicitly warns that interviewers often check the "passion" box after a polished answer, without noticing what the candidate's own questions reveal.
- The broader principle: "The way you read a person is by looking at what they do and what they say when there are no constraints." Candidate-generated questions are a lower-constraint, more authentic window into priorities.

## Use cases
- Hiring managers evaluating director-level or GPM-level PM candidates where passion for the domain is a key criterion.
- Any interviewer who suspects a candidate may be "performing" enthusiasm rather than genuinely feeling it.
- Calibrating interview structure — deciding when and how to open the floor to candidate questions so you can observe what they ask first.
- Debrief discussions where the team is split on a candidate who interviewed well on paper but something felt off.
- Candidates who want to signal genuine passion — this is equally useful as a coaching guide for what kinds of questions to lead with.

## Patterns & frameworks

**Questions-as-signal framework**
Rather than relying on answers to passion-oriented questions, flip attention to the questions the candidate volunteers. The first few unprompted questions a candidate asks reveal their actual priorities — self-interest vs. product/customer interest — without the filter of interview coaching.

**Constraint-removal heuristic**
"Read a person by what they do and say when there are no constraints." Scripted interview answers are high-constraint (candidates know what's wanted). The questions they choose to ask are low-constraint, making them a more authentic behavioral signal.

**Candidate A vs. Candidate B contrast**
A concrete comparison pattern: career-oriented questions (team size, comp trajectory, evaluation criteria) vs. product-oriented questions (customer gaps, competitive blind spots, strategic clarity). Neither set is wrong, but the *order and emphasis* of the first few questions reveals where the candidate's mind naturally goes.

**Skill-as-camouflage trap**
Senior PMs use persuasion and inspiration as core job skills. In an interview, they deploy those same skills in response to passion questions, making their answers genuinely impressive but misleading. Recognizing that a polished answer may be a job skill on display — not evidence of the underlying trait — is the key interviewer mindset shift.
