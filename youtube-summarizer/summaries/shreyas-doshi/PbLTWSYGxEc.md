# The Three Golden Questions to Ask a Founder

Video ID: `PbLTWSYGxEc`

## Summary
This video presents a framework of three high-impact questions candidates should ask founders or senior executives at the end of a job interview, specifically tailored for senior product roles (Head of Product, VP-level and above). The core argument is that most candidates waste their limited "do you have questions for me?" time with generic, low-signal questions that fail to differentiate them. By asking questions that make the interviewer think — and that signal the candidate is optimizing for the company rather than themselves — candidates stand out and gain genuinely useful information. The advice is most applicable when interviewing with a founder or senior executive, not for individual contributor PM roles reporting to a GPM at a large tech company.

## Key insights
- Most interviewers only leave ~5 minutes at the end for candidate questions, so every question must be high-leverage — you can't change the time allocation, only how you use it
- Common candidate questions (vision, culture, recent launches, ideal candidate profile, what does success look like) are described as "nonsense" — they're generic, low-signal, and asked by every other candidate
- Asking weak questions is a missed opportunity to differentiate yourself and have a meaningful peer-level conversation with a senior leader
- The three golden questions are designed to (1) make the interviewer think rather than recite a rehearsed answer, (2) signal you're optimizing for the company's success rather than your own position, and (3) generate genuinely useful intelligence for you as a candidate
- The ownership question directly addresses the #1 source of early failure in Head of Product roles: founder/exec conflict over who controls the product — surfacing this upfront can prevent a bad hire on both sides
- If a founder says they want to own nothing after hiring a Head of Product, the candidate is advised to push back constructively, noting the founder's deep product context has real value
- "What does your ideal candidate look like?" is called out as particularly weak — a savvy hiring manager may respond dismissively ("did you read the job description?")
- Framing questions around the company and the role — not around yourself — is the key signal that separates strong senior candidates from the rest

## Use cases
- Interviewing as a Head of Product, CPO, or VP of Product at a startup or growth-stage company
- Interviewing with a VP or senior exec at a large tech company (FAANG-level) where that exec is directly hiring for their team
- Any senior IC or leadership role where you have direct access to a founder or executive decision-maker during the interview process
- Situations where you want to convert a 5-minute Q&A into a deeper follow-up conversation
- **Not applicable:** PM3 or other individual contributor roles reporting to a GPM at a large company, where the hiring manager lacks product-level ownership visibility

## Patterns & frameworks
**The Three Golden Questions Framework**
A set of three interview questions designed to differentiate senior candidates. Each question replaces a common weak version with a more provocative, company-centric formulation:

| Weak version | Golden version |
|---|---|
| "What should you continue owning after hiring someone?" (implied) → usually not asked at all | "What aspects of the product makes sense for you to continue owning after you hire the Head of Product?" |
| "What does your ideal candidate look like?" | "What is the one superpower you want this person to have?" |
| "What does success look like in this role?" | "What are the one or two things that truly matter that this person needs to score an 11 out of 10 on?" |

**Optimize-for-the-company signaling**
A meta-principle underlying all three questions: the framing should make it clear you are asking what's right for the business, not negotiating personal ownership or scope. This positions you as a peer and strategic thinker rather than a self-interested job seeker.

**Make-them-think question design**
A heuristic for evaluating interview questions: a good question is one the interviewer hasn't been asked before and must genuinely pause to answer. Questions with rehearsed answers (vision, culture, success criteria) provide low signal; questions that require original thought reveal authentic priorities and generate useful intel.
