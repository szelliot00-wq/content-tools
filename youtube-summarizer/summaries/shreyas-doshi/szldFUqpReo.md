# The 2 Types of Stuckness for Product Managers

Video ID: `szldFUqpReo`

## Summary
This video by Shreyas Doshi addresses why some PMs advance rapidly while others stagnate, naming and analyzing two distinct career plateaus: "Level 1 Stuckness" (senior IC PMs who can't break into leadership) and "Level 2 Stuckness" (GPM/Director-level PMs who can't reach VP/CPO). The central argument is that career growth requires deliberate skill decomposition rather than vague feedback like "improve your product sense." The video presents the "10-30-50 PM" framework for escaping Level 1, and identifies strategy, communication, and editing as the three skills that keep PM leaders stuck at Level 2. It is most relevant to senior individual contributor PMs and GPM/Director-level PMs looking to accelerate their careers or break through a plateau.

## Key insights
- **Two named sticking points exist in PM careers**: Level 1 (senior IC → team lead/scope expansion) and Level 2 (GPM/Director → VP/CPO). Both are common enough to deserve names and targeted remedies.
- **The 10-30-50 PM framework**: An IC PM becomes "unstoppable" if they are top 10% in one dimension, top 30% in another, and top 50% in a third. The specific combination doesn't matter much — the pattern is what drives outsized outcomes in scope, promotions, and job offers.
- **Reaching the 10-30-50 threshold places you in the top 10% of compensation for your PM peer group**, an advantage that can compound for the rest of your career.
- **Decomposition is the core meta-skill for improvement**: To improve any skill, you must first break it into sub-skills and tackle each component. Without decomposition, feedback like "improve your product sense" is useless.
- **Product sense decomposes into three sub-skills**: cognitive empathy, domain knowledge, and creativity — each of which can be worked on specifically and further decomposed.
- **At Level 2, technical PM skills are table stakes**: Once you've held a GPM/Director role at a respectable company, your technical PM competency is assumed. The differentiator becomes influence.
- **Communication is a multiplier, not a standalone skill**: Great communication makes every other skill appear stronger. A PM who is "decent" at strategy but communicates brilliantly will be perceived as exceptional at strategy.
- **The three skills causing Level 2 Stuckness are strategy, communication, and editing** — derived from hundreds of observations across top companies over many years.
- **Editing is an integrative skill** (combining multiple sub-skills), which is why it's the hardest for new PM managers. The concept comes from Keith Rabois, who attributed it to Jack Dorsey at Square: great leaders are editors (cutting, simplifying, clarifying) rather than writers (creating everything themselves).
- **Switching from writer to editor is the hardest transition for new PM managers** — until they make it, their direct reports will be frustrated.
- **Learning style must be embraced, not fought**: The three practical learning styles are read/listen/watch, doing, and shadowing. Resistance to reading a book isn't a discipline problem — it signals a mismatch with your natural learning style.
- **Book recommendations are highly specific**: For strategy, *Understanding Michael Porter* (read it four times before asking for the next book). For communication, *Never Split the Difference*. Generic YouTube searches on "product strategy" are explicitly discouraged.

## Use cases
- A senior IC PM who keeps getting passed over for promotions or broader scope and doesn't understand why.
- A GPM or Director who is well-regarded at their company but can't land VP/CPO offers externally or get promoted internally.
- A PM who has received vague feedback ("be more strategic," "improve your product sense") with no actionable guidance on how to actually improve.
- A new PM manager struggling to delegate and let go of hands-on execution work.
- Any PM trying to build a structured, intentional career development plan rather than growing randomly.
- A PM who has tried to read leadership or strategy books but keeps abandoning them — and feels guilty about it.

## Patterns & frameworks

**Level 1 / Level 2 Stuckness**
Two named career plateaus. Level 1: senior IC PM who can't break into management or expanded scope. Level 2: GPM/Director who can't reach VP/CPO. Naming them makes diagnosis clearer and allows targeted interventions.

**The 10-30-50 PM**
An IC PM who is top 10% in one skill dimension, top 30% in a second, and top 50% in a third. The exact combination is flexible. This pattern is observed across company stages, sizes, and regions, and is described as the surest (though not only) path to product leadership and top-decile compensation.

**Skill Decomposition**
To improve any fuzzy skill, break it into concrete sub-skills first, then address each sub-skill independently. Example: product sense → cognitive empathy + domain knowledge + creativity. This creates clarity on what to actually practice and learn, turning vague feedback into an actionable plan.

**Writer vs. Editor**
A leadership mental model (attributed to Jack Dorsey via Keith Rabois): early-career PMs are "writers" who create and execute directly. Senior PM leaders must shift to being "editors" — taking others' work and cutting, clarifying, and simplifying it rather than doing it themselves. Failure to make this shift frustrates teams and blocks advancement.

**Three Learning Styles**
A simplified taxonomy: (1) Read/Listen/Watch, (2) Do, (3) Shadow. Each person has a dominant style. Fighting your natural style causes resistance and stalled learning; embracing it allows you to select the right learning modality (books, hands-on projects, mentorship/observation) for each skill you're developing.

**Communication as Multiplier**
Communication is framed not as a soft add-on but as a force multiplier on all other skills. A PM who is good (not great) at strategy, execution, or analytics will appear great at those things if their communication is excellent. This makes it the highest-leverage single skill to invest in at the leadership level.
