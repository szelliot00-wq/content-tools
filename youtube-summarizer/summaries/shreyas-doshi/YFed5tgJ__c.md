# How to Actually Choose Between Job Offers

Video ID: `YFed5tgJ__c`

## Summary
This video, presented by Shreyas Doshi (a product leader), offers a structured framework for choosing between job offers — and argues that most people optimize for the wrong factors. The core claim is that title, money, and scope attract you to a job but won't keep you happy, while people, culture, and growth determine long-term fulfillment. Doshi walks through a three-stage decision process called the Joy-Ego-Table Stakes framework, illustrates it with his own 2015 job search (which led him to Stripe), and provides tactical advice on assessing talent quality during interviews. It is most relevant to experienced product managers and senior individual contributors or leaders who are evaluating multiple competitive offers.

## Key insights
- **The core trap:** People join companies for ego factors (title, money, scope) but stay — or leave — because of joy factors (people, culture, growth). Optimizing for ego at the decision point is the most common and costly mistake in career-making.
- **Hedonic adaptation kills ego motivation:** Within one month of joining, the excitement around a fancy title, compensation, or scope fades. You cannot sustain motivation by repeatedly looking at your pay stub — it is human nature. What remains is the daily reality of the culture and the people around you.
- **The three-stage decision model — Reject, Narrow, Accept:** First, reject companies that fail your table stakes (mission/interest, impact, work-life harmony). Second, narrow the remaining pool to two or three using ego factors (money, scope, title) ranked by a spreadsheet. Third, pick among the finalists based purely on joy — and at this stage, throw the spreadsheet away.
- **Don't join based on table stakes:** You should eliminate companies that don't meet your table stakes, but you should never *accept* an offer just because a company's mission resonates or feels impressive to others. Making decisions to look good at cocktail parties is explicitly called out as a trap.
- **The Gut Simulation exercise:** When confused between two or three finalists, spend 10 minutes per company vividly imagining what it would actually feel like to work there — presenting to colleagues, solving problems at a whiteboard, walking into the office daily. The company that feels best at the end of that 30-minute exercise is the right choice. Doshi credits this technique to an executive recruiter and says it gave him clarity to choose Stripe over companies that ranked higher on his spreadsheet.
- **Joe Kraus's simplification:** "Narrow based on analytical factors, pick based on gut." A one-line distillation of the same framework.
- **Ultimate simplification:** When in doubt, pick the place with the best people. Everything else flows from the quality of the people you work with.
- **Doshi's 2015 offer example:** He had offers from multiple companies including Airbnb and Stripe. He eliminated some for work-life balance (table stakes), eliminated Airbnb because the equity upside wasn't sufficient for his goals at the time (ego stage), and then chose Stripe through the gut simulation exercise — even though Stripe ranked lower on his spreadsheet than the other two finalists.
- **Assessing talent quality during interviews — six signals:**
  1. Quality of *follow-up* questions (not scripted questions) — these reveal real-time reasoning ability.
  2. Overall rigor of the hiring process — too easy is a red flag because the same low bar applies to everyone else they hire.
  3. LinkedIn research and Glassdoor reviews.
  4. Extensive reverse interviews — for senior roles, request conversations with 7–8 people out of genuine curiosity about culture and challenges, not as a power move.
  5. Ask the hiring manager to introduce you to the 3–4 best people at the company across all functions — this gives you a caliber benchmark and, if you join, pre-built relationships with top performers.
  6. Assess execution in their products — insight, pace, and quality of output. Talent must manifest in differentiated products, market traction, and thoughtful execution, not just pixel perfection.
- **Script for buying more time:** When offers don't align in timing, use this language: *"Your company is definitely among my top two to three choices. I want to ask for more time because whatever company I join next, I want to mentally commit to it for five to seven years. On that horizon, it doesn't make sense to rush things just to save a few weeks. Do you think this is reasonable?"* This reframes the ask in terms the hiring manager respects and will almost never say no to — and makes you look more, not less, impressive.

## Use cases
- A senior PM, director, or VP evaluating two or three simultaneous competing offers and feeling paralyzed by the decision.
- Any professional (not just PMs) choosing between companies after passing the interview stage at multiple places.
- Someone tempted to join a company primarily because of prestige, mission hype, or what it will look like to peers.
- A candidate who needs to stall one company's deadline while waiting for another offer to finalize.
- Someone doing due diligence on company culture and talent quality before accepting an offer.
- Professionals weighing internal promotions or internal role transfers against external opportunities.
- Entry-to-senior-level candidates learning how to run a structured reverse interview process (with the caveat that some tactics like extensive reverse interviews are better suited to mid-to-senior roles).

## Patterns & frameworks

**Joy-Ego-Table Stakes Framework**
A three-layer decision model for evaluating job offers. Each layer has defined factors and a specific role in the decision:
- *Table Stakes* (Interest/mission, Impact, Harmony/work-life balance): Use these to *eliminate* companies that don't meet a baseline. Never use them to accept.
- *Ego* (Title, Money, Scope): Use a spreadsheet to stack-rank and narrow finalists to 2–3 options.
- *Joy* (People, Culture, Growth): Use gut feel — not spreadsheets — to make the final pick among the shortlist. Joy is what determines long-term satisfaction and whether you'll regret the decision.

**Reject → Narrow → Accept**
The procedural sequence that operationalizes the Joy-Ego-Table Stakes framework: reject on table stakes, narrow on ego, accept on joy.

**The Gut Simulation Exercise**
A 30-minute mental exercise where you spend ~10 minutes per finalist company vividly imagining daily working life there — meetings, whiteboard sessions, interactions with specific colleagues and founders. The goal is to bypass analytical rationalization and access an intuitive assessment of cultural and interpersonal fit. Useful specifically at the joy-level decision, after the spreadsheet phase is complete.

**The "Best People" Heuristic**
A further simplification: when all else is equal or you're still uncertain after the full framework, choose the company with the best people. Talent quality is treated as a leading indicator of both personal growth and company success.

**Talent Assessment Signal Hierarchy**
A repeatable process for evaluating people quality at a prospective employer during interviews: follow-up question quality → hiring process rigor → desk research → reverse interviews → "introduce me to your best 3–4 people" ask → product execution audit (insight, pace, quality).

**The Time Extension Script**
A reusable negotiation message for requesting offer deadline extensions without damaging relationships. Anchors the ask in a 5–7 year commitment framing, making the request sound disciplined and serious rather than indecisive.
