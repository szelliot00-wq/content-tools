# How to Test for Mindset in Interviews

Video ID: `FkWl1TsObcE`

## Summary
This video is a practical guide for hiring managers and founders on how to detect genuine product passion and other mindset qualities in PM candidates during interviews. The core argument is that sophisticated candidates will always give impressive answers to direct questions about their interests, so interviewers must instead read the unconstrained signals candidates emit — especially the questions candidates choose to ask. The speaker draws a sharp contrast between candidates who ask self-oriented questions (team size, growth trajectory, evaluation criteria) versus product-oriented questions (customer gaps, competitive positioning, strategy clarity). It is most relevant to hiring managers, founders, and interview panel members evaluating mid-level to senior PMs.

## Key insights
- Most interviewers never explicitly list the mindset factors they are testing for, even privately — this alone is a significant gap in the typical hiring process.
- Asking "What makes you interested in this role?" does not effectively test passion; experienced PMs are skilled at crafting inspiring, persuasive answers because that is literally their job.
- The questions a candidate chooses to ask — when there are no constraints on what they can say — are a far more reliable signal of their true mindset than their answers to your questions.
- Candidate A (self-oriented questions: team size, cross-functional partners, growth prospects, evaluation criteria) vs. Candidate B (product-oriented questions: where the product falls short, unaddressed use cases, strategic clarity) illustrates the contrast clearly — A looks thoughtful but reveals weak product passion; B reveals genuine domain interest.
- Self-oriented questions are not inherently bad; candidates do need that information before accepting a job. The tell is when those questions dominate the *first* set of questions in an early conversation.
- A four-to-six week interview process creates a natural test: measure the delta in the candidate's product knowledge from start to finish. A passionate candidate will naturally keep researching because they find it enjoyable, producing a meaningful knowledge delta. A disengaged candidate will show little or none.
- The general principle: read people by what they do and say when there are no constraints, not when they are responding to structured prompts.
- Mindset issues are hard or impossible to coach and must change on the candidate's own timeline — so identifying them before hiring matters more than identifying skill gaps, which are more coachable.
- Diagnosing mindset fully in interviews is not realistic, but testing for targeted, specific mindset factors is achievable if you define them in advance.

## Use cases
- Hiring managers evaluating mid-level to senior PMs or director/GPM-level candidates for product roles.
- Founders building early product teams where passion and product orientation are especially critical.
- Interview panel members who want to move beyond resume and answer quality as the primary evaluation signals.
- Anyone writing job descriptions that mention "passion for the problem" but have no corresponding interview mechanism to actually test for it.
- Teams conducting multi-week interview loops who want to use the full timeline as an evaluation tool, not just discrete interview sessions.
- Situations where a candidate is impressive on paper and articulate in responses but something feels off — this framework provides a structured way to investigate that gut feeling.

## Patterns & frameworks

**The Unconstrained Signal Test**
Read candidates by observing what they say and do when there are no prompts or constraints guiding them. The clearest window into mindset is the questions candidates voluntarily choose to ask, because nothing is directing their choice. Self-oriented first questions signal career/status mindset; product-oriented first questions signal domain passion.

**Candidate A vs. Candidate B Contrast**
A mental model for categorizing the nature of candidate questions. Candidate A questions center on org structure, team growth, cross-functional partners, and personal evaluation criteria. Candidate B questions center on customer needs, competitive gaps, unaddressed use cases, and product strategy. Both sets of questions are legitimate, but the first questions a candidate gravitates to reveal their natural orientation.

**Define Mindset Factors Before Interviewing**
Before any interview process, explicitly list (even privately) the top two or three mindset factors you are screening for — e.g., passion for the domain, customer empathy, ownership orientation. Without this list, you have no structured way to observe or interpret signals related to mindset.

**Knowledge Delta Test**
Over a multi-week interview process, track how much the candidate's understanding of your product, customers, and competitive landscape grows from the first conversation to the last. A high delta indicates natural curiosity and self-driven research. A flat or low delta indicates low genuine interest, regardless of what the candidate says about their passion.

**Passion ≠ Articulate Answer to "Why Are You Interested?"**
A direct corollary of the unconstrained signal test: the ability to give an inspiring answer about passion is a PM skill, not evidence of passion itself. Testing for passion requires indirect, observational methods rather than direct questions.
