# How to Get Promoted

Video ID: `2dkbuOSQoAM`

## Summary
This video lays out a practical, systems-level framework for how professional recognition and promotion actually work inside companies. The presenter, Shreyas (likely Shreyas Doshi, a well-known PM leader), argues that recognition is a function of four variables — scope, outcomes, outputs, and visibility — and that understanding how your specific company weights those variables is prerequisite to advancing your career. He then goes deep on two adjacent topics: how to evaluate internal transfer opportunities (with particular focus on choosing a manager), and how to "manage up" using a structured working-backwards promotion plan co-created with your manager. The video is most relevant to mid-to-senior individual contributors and product managers who are actively thinking about promotion or career inflection points.

---

## Key insights

- **Recognition has a formula:** Recognition = f(scope, outcomes, outputs, visibility). These four variables determine how you are seen and rewarded, regardless of company or industry.
- **Company culture changes the weighting:** Some companies (especially larger or more political ones) weight scope and visibility heavily over outcomes and outputs. Startups tend to weight scope and outcomes. A purely visibility-based culture is a red flag — Shreyas says he wouldn't recommend joining one.
- **Major career inflections come from one of four things:** (1) a career/function change, (2) joining a new high-growth company, (3) taking on new responsibility/ownership, or (4) — rarely — a transformative new manager. Understanding this helps you engineer your own inflection.
- **Internal timing is never perfect:** There will never be an ideal moment to take on new scope or an internal transfer. You must stay open to opportunities even when timing feels wrong.
- **The two factors that matter most for internal role evaluation** are (1) the priority and strategic importance of the initiative you'd own, and (2) your prospective manager. Everything else is secondary.
- **The manager spectrum is not binary:** Most people fall into binary thinking — "great manager" vs. "toxic manager." The real continuum is: great → competent → overwhelmed → hit-or-miss → absentee → counterproductive → toxic. Only the last two warrant rejection.
- **Your manager tolerance range should change based on context:** When joining a *new company*, your range can be wide (overwhelmed and absentee managers are acceptable because the company variable is what you're optimizing). When doing an *internal transfer*, the company is fixed, so the manager variable becomes far more important and your acceptable range should narrow significantly.
- **You can delegate up:** It's not only managers who delegate to ICs. Strong PMs can and should delegate things they dislike or aren't best suited for up to their manager.
- **Five criteria for evaluating a manager (for top ~10-20% PMs):**
  1. How does this manager make it easier for me to get things done?
  2. What can I delegate *to* this manager?
  3. Can they recognize that I'm top-tier talent?
  4. What is their credibility in the org?
  5. Will they advocate strongly for me across the company?
- **Managing up requires treating your manager as an ally, not an adversary.** Don't corner them with "when will you promote me?" — that creates pressure and friction.
- **The exact script for initiating a promotion conversation:** *"I want to thank you for agreeing to be a close partner in helping me navigate to the next level. I find it helpful to keep myself accountable towards achieving what it will take to get there. I was thinking of creating a quick, very approximate view of what needs to happen between now and the next perf cycle to increase my odds of getting promoted. Can I create a brief working backwards plan and get your input on it in our next one-on-one?"* — Every word is deliberate. It signals ownership (not blame), acknowledges gaps, and frames it as collaborative. A manager cannot realistically say no.
- **Surprises at promo committees are avoidable:** Most post-committee rejections ("your scope wasn't big enough," "your impact wasn't visible") could have been surfaced and addressed months earlier. The working backwards plan is the tool to do this.
- **The Promotion Drivers Template** has four main sections mapped directly to the recognition formula: Scope Managed, Output Shipped, Outcomes Driven, and Visibility Provided. It works equally well for targeting a higher performance rating, not just a formal promotion.

## Use cases

- A mid-level PM who has been at their company for 1–2 years and wants to understand why they haven't been promoted despite strong output.
- A senior IC preparing for a promo cycle who wants to proactively partner with their manager rather than wait and hope.
- Someone evaluating an internal transfer offer and trying to decide whether to take it.
- Someone joining a new company who wants a framework for assessing their prospective manager without over-indexing on manager quality.
- A manager who wants to better support their direct reports' career growth and reduce promo-cycle surprises.
- Someone at a company where "visibility" seems disproportionately rewarded and trying to understand why — and whether to stay.
- Any IC who has ever received vague feedback like "your scope isn't big enough" *after* a promo committee decision and wants to prevent that from happening again.

## Patterns & frameworks

**Recognition Formula**
Recognition = f(scope, outcomes, outputs, visibility). The core mental model of the video. Scope is the breadth/importance of what you own. Outputs are the tangible things you ship. Outcomes are the measurable impact of those outputs. Visibility is whether decision-makers can see all of the above. Every career strategy action should move at least one of these variables.

**Company Culture Weighting Matrix**
Not all four recognition variables are weighted equally everywhere. Startups tend to weight scope + outcomes; large/political companies often weight scope + visibility. Purely visibility-based companies are red flags. Before optimizing your career at a company, diagnose which variables it actually rewards.

**Career Inflection Point Model**
Major career acceleration comes from: function change, new company (high-growth), new responsibility/ownership, or a transformative manager. Recognizing which lever is available to you — and acting on it — is how to engineer a step change rather than incremental growth.

**Manager Continuum**
A seven-point spectrum from great → competent → overwhelmed → hit-or-miss → absentee → counterproductive → toxic. Replaces the false binary of "great vs. toxic." Used to calibrate how permissive to be when evaluating a role.

**Context-Dependent Manager Tolerance**
When joining a new company: wide acceptable range (you're optimizing for company fit). When doing an internal transfer: narrow acceptable range (company is fixed, so manager quality becomes the dominant variable). Pure logic, not opinion.

**Managing Up Script**
A specific, word-for-word conversational template for opening a promotion-planning partnership with your manager. Key moves: (1) place accountability on yourself, (2) acknowledge gaps explicitly, (3) frame promotion as increasing odds — not a demand, and (4) ask only for input on a plan, not a commitment. Designed to make it psychologically impossible for a manager to say no.

**Promotion Drivers Template (Working Backwards Plan)**
A structured document co-created with your manager that maps your promotion path against the four recognition variables. Four sections: Scope Managed, Output Shipped, Outcomes Driven, Visibility Provided. Purpose: surface ambiguities and misalignments early so they can be addressed before a promo committee meets — not after. Applicable to both promotion and performance rating goals.
