# How to Communicate to Get Recognized

Video ID: `WgFAKCEpEsE`

## Summary
This video argues that competence alone does not automatically build a professional reputation — it must be actively translated through communication, manager advocacy, and peer perception. The speaker, drawing on personal career mistakes and experience leading PMs at companies like Stripe, lays out a set of communication principles for product managers who want to be recognized by senior leadership. The core framework is that how you communicate your work is often more than 50% of how you are judged, even if communication is only 5–10% of your actual job. The advice is most relevant to product managers at any level, particularly those who are technically strong but struggling to gain visibility or influence with executives.

## Key insights
- **Competence does not automatically become reputation.** It only converts via three channels: (1) your own active communication of your insight, execution, and impact; (2) your manager's advocacy; (3) your peers' perception of you.
- **Match your communication superpower to your company's culture.** Written-culture companies (e.g., Amazon, Stripe) reward top-5% writers. Being a great speaker is a liability there. Leaders at Stripe with impressive resumes failed to gain influence because writing wasn't their superpower — and "top 5%" means best out of 20 people, a genuinely high bar.
- **Overprepare for high-stakes communication moments.** If you have a major product review on Thursday, drop sprint planning, delay Slack responses, and spend the full four days preparing. Communication may be 5–10% of your job but over 50% of how you're judged.
- **Communicate at their level, not yours.** Effective communicators simulate the meeting in vivid detail beforehand — predict the CEO's questions, the VP of Engineering's objections, every likely pushback. The speaker would literally role-play full meetings with PMs before the real ones, and the actual meetings would unfold nearly identically.
- **Operate at the impact level, not the execution level.** There are three levels of product work: optics, execution, and impact. PMs tend to present at the execution level (e.g., "David is on parental leave so we pushed to April"); executives care about the impact level (will this launch move the metric we care about?). Talking at the execution level in front of a senior executive signals you don't understand what actually matters.
- **Be concise and dispassionate.** Dispassionate doesn't mean emotionless — it means emotionally consistent and controlled. Unpredictable emotional behavior undermines the perception of senior leadership material. Long-winded answers to direct executive questions lose the room.
- **Follow up exquisitely.** Send notes and action items after meetings. When you owe an answer, deliver a concise, specific response. Most people don't do this at a high level.
- **Don't BS executives.** BSing may go undetected with peers or reports, but executives have seen it thousands of times and spot it immediately. The analogy used: a kindergartner thinks they're fooling the teacher by stealing a cookie — they never are.
- **Run your own informal performance cycle.** Don't wait for a company-wide perf cycle, especially if you're 3–6 months into a role or a relationship has been difficult. Proactively seek structured feedback.
- **Communication is learnable.** Recommended books: *The Charisma Myth* by Olivia Fox Cabane and *Never Split the Difference* by Chris Voss.

## Use cases
- A PM who consistently delivers strong work but isn't getting promoted or recognized by leadership.
- A PM preparing for a high-stakes product review, strategy presentation, or executive demo.
- A PM who just joined a new company (3–6 months in) and wants to calibrate their communication style to the culture.
- A PM or leader evaluating whether their communication superpower (speaking vs. writing) fits their current company's primary influence channel.
- A PM manager or director coaching junior PMs before important meetings with senior leadership.
- Anyone preparing for a difficult conversation with an executive where pushback is likely.
- A PM who tends to over-explain execution details when asked strategic or outcome-focused questions.

## Patterns & frameworks

**Three Channels of Reputation**
Competence converts to reputation only through: (1) your active communication of insight, execution, and impact; (2) your manager's advocacy; (3) peer perception. All three require deliberate effort — none happen passively.

**Three Levels of Product Work: Optics → Execution → Impact**
A framework for diagnosing communication misalignment. Most PMs default to the execution level in presentations (schedules, team constraints, milestones). Executives filter at the impact level (what outcome does this create?). Effective communication requires leading with impact and using execution details only when asked or as supporting evidence.

**Meeting Simulation / Vivid Pre-Mortem**
Before any high-stakes meeting, mentally (or with a coach/manager) play out the entire meeting in sequence: what you'll say, what each stakeholder will ask, what objections arise, what will land well. The speaker used this with PMs directly — role-playing the CEO, VP Eng, and others — and found the real meetings tracked the simulation closely. Borrowed from how sports coaches prepare for championship games.

**Superpower-Culture Fit Test**
Identify whether your company's primary influence channel (written vs. verbal) matches your strongest communication mode. If it doesn't, either invest heavily in developing the weaker skill to top-5% level or factor it into company/role selection decisions.

**Exquisite Follow-Up Loop**
After any meaningful meeting: send notes and action items promptly; explicitly close open loops ("you asked X, here's my concise answer"). Treated as a repeatable discipline rather than a one-off courtesy.
