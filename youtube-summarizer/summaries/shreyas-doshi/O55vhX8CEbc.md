# The LinkedIn Envy and the Impact Lie

Video ID: `O55vhX8CEbc`

## Summary
This video is an excerpt from a talk by Shreyas Doshi (referred to as "I" in the transcript) aimed at product managers and tech professionals about the self-deceptive mental patterns that drive poor career decisions. The core argument is that smart people are especially susceptible to rationalizing irrational decisions — they make emotionally driven choices and then construct logical-sounding justifications after the fact. Two specific self-deception traps are examined in detail: "LinkedIn Envy" and the "Impact Lie." The content is most relevant to mid-to-senior tech professionals (PMs, directors, executives) evaluating career moves or job changes.

## Key insights
- **LinkedIn Envy is a career decision trap**: Seeing a former colleague (e.g., "Bob") land a flashy CPO role triggers comparison and mimicry — people decide they want the same title without examining whether that person's life is actually fulfilling or what they sacrificed for it.
- **You want Bob's LinkedIn, not Bob's life**: The critical error is wanting someone's external credentials while assuming it comes packaged with your own existing quality of life — that's not how it works in reality.
- **Smart people justify dumb decisions better**: The more intelligent someone is, the more convincing the post-hoc rationalization. A decision driven by LinkedIn envy gets dressed up as "I want more ownership" or some other principled-sounding reason.
- **"I want to make an impact" is the most common interview answer**: Shreyas estimates roughly 3 out of 4 people say this when asked what they're looking for in their next role.
- **The math on startup impact doesn't add up**: If your real goal is impact on the world, joining a 25- or 100-person startup gives you near-zero odds of achieving that. A small role at Google, SpaceX, or a comparable scaled organization statistically produces more real-world impact.
- **Saying "impact" is a social script, not an authentic belief**: You can't say "I want to get rich" in an interview — even OpenAI's founder publicly claims money isn't his goal — so "impact" became the socially acceptable answer everyone is expected to give.
- **Repetition turns a lie into a belief**: Saying the impact line in interviews, college essays, and professional conversations over and over eventually convinces people it's a core part of their identity, when it's actually a conditioned response.
- **The conditioning starts early**: College application essays requiring students to perform altruism ("I started a nonprofit...") plant the seed, and years of reinforcement in professional settings cement it.
- **The discomfort is a signal**: People who feel defensive hearing this ("No, but I really do want to make an impact") are likely the ones most thoroughly conditioned by this pattern.

## Use cases
- A senior PM or director feeling restless after seeing a peer get promoted to VP or CPO — evaluating whether the desire to chase that title is genuine or LinkedIn-driven.
- Anyone preparing for job interviews who wants to answer "what are you looking for?" more honestly and strategically.
- Professionals considering leaving a stable large-company role for an early-stage startup, who should stress-test their stated reasons.
- Career coaches or hiring managers trying to get authentic answers from candidates rather than rehearsed "impact" language.
- Anyone doing an annual or quarterly career reflection who wants to filter out socially-conditioned goals from genuine ones.
- Tech professionals at inflection points (layoffs, promotions, acquisition) who risk making reactive decisions based on peer comparison.

## Patterns & frameworks
**LinkedIn Envy**
A cognitive trap where observing a peer's career milestone on LinkedIn triggers irrational career decisions driven by status comparison rather than self-knowledge. The pattern: see peer achievement → feel inadequate → decide to pursue the same title/role → rationalize the decision with plausible-sounding logic. The fix is to interrogate what that person actually sacrificed and whether their life — not just their profile — is one you'd want.

**The Impact Lie (Self-deception through repetition)**
A social script ("I want to make an impact") that begins as an expected interview performance but becomes an internalized belief through repeated use. The mechanism mirrors propaganda: say something often enough and you believe it. The framework for auditing it is simple math — if impact on the world is truly the goal, does the vehicle (e.g., a small startup) actually maximize the odds of that outcome? If not, the stated goal and the chosen action are misaligned, which reveals the real driver is something else (status, money, fear, excitement).

**Post-hoc rationalization by intelligence**
A meta-pattern underlying both traps: intelligent people are better at generating convincing justifications for decisions they've already emotionally made. The smarter the person, the more sophisticated the excuse — which makes self-deception harder to catch. Awareness of this pattern is a prerequisite for making more honest career decisions.
