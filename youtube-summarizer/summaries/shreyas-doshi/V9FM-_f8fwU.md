# Why You Shouldn't Ignore Money

Video ID: `V9FM-_f8fwU`

## Summary
This video is a short, direct argument by a product management thought leader (based in the San Francisco Bay Area) for why professionals — especially those in tech — should not dismiss or deprioritize money when making career decisions. The speaker pushes back against a prevalent Silicon Valley attitude that equates caring about money with being a "mercenary," calling out the hypocrisy of founders and VCs who hold that view while openly celebrating fundraising milestones. The core framework is a stack-ranking of three career variables — money, scope, and title — with the argument that money is the most durable of the three. The video is most relevant to product managers, tech workers, and early-career professionals navigating compensation and career trade-off decisions.

## Key insights
- The speaker explicitly refuses to dictate anyone's personal career priorities ("I cannot tell you what your stack rank should be — it's your life"), but argues that ignoring money entirely is a logical mistake.
- The "mercenary" label commonly used in Silicon Valley startup culture to shame people who prioritize compensation is hypocritical: the same founders who use it publicly celebrate Series C raises (e.g., "$100M at a $2B valuation"), and the entire VC business model is fundamentally about returning money to limited partners.
- This anti-money attitude is described as more geographically concentrated — prevalent in parts of the US and Europe, but largely absent in Asia, where the speaker says the audience would find the premise of this argument self-evident.
- **Reason 1:** Money is necessary to have a chance at a comfortable life for yourself and those you love, if you participate in modern civilization. Even idealistic alternatives (e.g., off-grid farming) don't eliminate life's financial emergencies (illness, unexpected crises).
- **Reason 2:** Of the three career variables — money, scope, and title — money is the most durable. Title disappears the moment you leave a company (it persists on LinkedIn but holds no operational weight). Scope can vanish at your *current* company the moment a reorg happens. Money, once earned, stays with you.
- **Reason 3 (bonus):** Financial security enables you to take more career and life risks. If you are not financially stressed, you have the freedom to pursue bolder moves, whereas chasing only scope and title leaves you more vulnerable and constrained.

## Use cases
- A mid-career PM evaluating two offers: one with a higher title/scope but lower comp, another with better pay but a less prestigious role.
- An early-career professional in a startup being told that equity, mission, or "learning opportunity" should outweigh salary.
- Anyone being pressured by startup culture to treat compensation negotiation as a character flaw or sign of disloyalty.
- A PM or IC who has been through a reorg and lost scope/title, reassessing what career assets are actually stable.
- Someone building a financial safety net specifically so they can afford to take a risk (career change, startup, leave of absence) in the future.
- Professionals in Europe or the US who have absorbed cultural messaging that deprioritizes financial ambition.

## Patterns & frameworks
**The Career Stack Rank (Money / Scope / Title)**
A mental model for evaluating job offers and career moves by ranking three variables: money, scope (the breadth and impact of your work), and title (your seniority designation). The speaker doesn't prescribe a universal ranking but argues that any rational stack rank should account for money's unique property of *durability* — it outlasts both scope (vulnerable to reorgs) and title (resets at each company change). The implicit recommendation is to weight money more heavily than most tech workers in certain cultures currently do.

**Durability Test**
A sub-framework embedded in the stack rank: when comparing career assets, ask how long each one lasts under adverse conditions. Title fails the durability test at company exit. Scope fails it at reorg. Money passes it because it transfers with you and persists regardless of organizational changes.

**Financial Security as an Enabler of Risk-Taking**
A compounding logic: earning and securing money is not the *end goal* but rather a *prerequisite* for having the freedom to take bigger risks later. This reframes financial ambition not as greed but as building optionality — the more financially secure you are, the less constrained your future decisions become.
