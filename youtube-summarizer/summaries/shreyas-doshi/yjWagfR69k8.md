# The Hiring Mistake That Cost Me the Most

Video ID: `yjWagfR69k8`

## Summary
A senior product leader reflects on a costly belief they held for much of their career: that any PM could be coached to grow in any skill, given the right manager and environment. The speaker argues this belief was rooted not in genuine faith in people, but in personal ego — the conviction that "if I can do it, anyone can." Through repeated hiring mistakes, they learned two hard truths: people grow on their own timeline, not yours, and they grow in directions they are naturally drawn to, not directions you push them toward. The video is most relevant to PM leaders and hiring managers, but the lessons apply broadly to anyone who manages people or makes hiring decisions.

## Key insights
- The speaker's core hiring mistake was suppressing their gut instinct about a candidate's flaw and rationalizing it away with "I can coach them through this."
- The belief that "everyone can grow in any area with the right environment" is heavily reinforced by LinkedIn culture, which frames failure to grow as an environmental problem rather than an individual one — letting people off the hook.
- The speaker's conviction was strengthened by their own significant personal growth in PM skills, which led to an immature and inaccurate generalization: "If I could do it, anyone should be able to."
- The real driver behind overlooking candidate flaws was ego — specifically the desire to prove oneself as a transformational manager — not genuine belief in the candidate's potential.
- Candidates hired despite identified flaws would typically perform adequately at first, but within a few months the same flaw would clearly limit their impact.
- Coaching rarely worked because the employees never opted into it. They said yes to coaching due to power dynamics (the speaker was their manager), but treated sessions as a checkbox rather than genuine development.
- People do not grow on your timeline — they grow on their own. And they grow in directions they are naturally attracted to, not directions their manager is pushing.
- Hiring someone despite a meaningful flaw in a role-critical area is unfair to three parties: the company, the hiring manager, and the candidate themselves.
- Lack of empathy was a compounding factor — when ego is dominant ("look how great I am as a manager"), it crowds out the capacity to genuinely understand another person's situation and natural trajectory.

## Use cases
- **Hiring managers** evaluating candidates who have strong suits but a notable gap in a skill critical to the role.
- **PM leaders** deciding whether to extend an offer to a promising candidate they believe they can "fix" post-hire.
- **Engineering or design managers** who are tempted to hire for potential over fit and plan to close gaps through 1:1 coaching.
- **Anyone managing underperformers** who were hired with known weaknesses — to reset expectations about coaching timelines and outcomes.
- **Self-reflection for leaders** who notice they have a pattern of hiring mistakes and want to diagnose the root cause.
- **New managers** susceptible to the "I can grow anyone" belief before it costs them multiple cycles of bad hires.

## Patterns & frameworks

**The Ego-Driven Hiring Trap**
The speaker identifies a loop: (1) notice a flaw in a candidate, (2) suppress the instinct to pass, (3) rationalize that the flaw is coachable, (4) hire anyway, (5) watch the flaw limit the person's impact, (6) enter coaching mode, (7) see coaching fail because the employee never truly opted in. The trap is sustained by the manager's ego, not their belief in the candidate.

**Opted-In vs. Assigned Growth**
A key mental model: coaching only works when the person being coached has chosen it for themselves. Growth assigned by a manager — go shadow this person, take this training, do these sessions — tends to be treated as a compliance checkbox. Real development happens when the individual is internally motivated and self-directed.

**Two Laws of Human Growth**
The speaker distills their learning into two principles:
1. People grow on *their own timeline*, not yours.
2. People grow in directions they are *naturally pulled toward*, not directions you push them.

These two laws directly undermine the case for hiring someone with a critical gap and expecting to close it through management.

**Environmental Attribution Bias (LinkedIn Effect)**
The speaker calls out a cultural pattern where growth failures are attributed to environment (bad manager, wrong company, lack of opportunity) rather than the individual. This framing is comfortable and socially rewarded (more likes), but it obscures personal accountability and reinforces the false belief that any gap can be closed with the right conditions.
