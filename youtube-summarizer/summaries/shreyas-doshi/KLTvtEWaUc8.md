# How to Position Your Product: An Example of How I Positioned my Product Sense Course

Video ID: `KLTvtEWaUc8`

## Summary
This short video shows how a product sense course creator worked through the positioning of his cohort-based course. The core argument is that most product and product sense training falls into process-, story-, or framework-oriented categories, and that deliberately choosing a different axis — skill-oriented teaching — creates a meaningful differentiator. The creator explains how that single positioning decision cascaded into course structure, daily prompts, and landing page copy. It is most relevant to course creators, educators, and product managers thinking about how to differentiate an educational product.

## Key insights
- The creator used a two-axis positioning framework: one axis being passive vs. interactive (he always knew he'd choose interactive, given a cohort-based format), and the other axis being the harder question of *how* the teaching would be delivered.
- Most product/product sense training clusters around three modes: **process-oriented** (follow these steps), **story-oriented** (here's what Company X did), and **framework-oriented** (here are the mental models). Recognizing this cluster took him several weeks.
- He deliberately positioned his course as **skill-oriented** — the goal is that students actually build the skill of product sense *during* the course, not just receive information about how to develop it.
- This single positioning choice had downstream effects on three things: (1) how he structured each day of the course, (2) the prompts he gave students throughout the day, and (3) how he framed the course on the landing page.
- The landing page explicitly calls out what the course is *not* — it won't rely on processes, playbooks, tactics, or frameworks — to reinforce the skill-building positioning.
- He is careful to clarify that process/story/framework approaches are legitimate; the point is simply to have *clarity* on your own approach, not to disparage alternatives.
- A co-presenter illustrates the practical difference: a skill-oriented course on product sense doesn't have the instructor dissect products *in front of* students — it has students dissect products themselves. The instructor designs for active student practice, not passive observation.

## Use cases
- **Course creators** deciding how to differentiate a new online or cohort-based course in a crowded category.
- **Product managers** who need to position a product where competitors cluster around similar features or approaches, and need to find a meaningful axis of differentiation.
- **Educators or trainers** designing a curriculum who want to be intentional about the student experience at each moment, not just the content delivered.
- **Landing page writers** who need to translate a positioning decision into clear, specific copy that signals what a product is *not*, as much as what it is.
- **Anyone building in a content or education market** where "more frameworks" or "more content" is the default competitive move.

## Patterns & frameworks
**Two-axis positioning map**
Plot your product on two axes to find white space. One axis should be a dimension where your product category naturally clusters (e.g., passive vs. interactive). The second, harder axis is where you find your real differentiator. The point is to identify where competitors cluster and consciously place yourself elsewhere.

**Teaching modality spectrum**
A mental model for categorizing educational products: process-oriented (step-by-step instructions), story-oriented (case studies and examples), framework-oriented (mental models and taxonomies), and skill-oriented (students practice and build capability directly). Most products in a category default to the first three; skill-oriented is the less common, more differentiated position.

**Positioning → experience → copy cascade**
Once a positioning decision is made, it should flow downstream consistently: first into the *experience design* (how each session or interaction is structured), then into *in-product prompts* (what you ask users/students to do), and finally into *marketing copy* (how you frame it on the landing page). Positioning is not just a marketing exercise — it constrains and informs product design.

**Active vs. passive delivery test**
A practical check for skill-oriented teaching: ask whether the instructor is doing the thing *in front of* the learner, or whether the learner is doing the thing themselves. Demonstrating product dissection = passive/framework mode. Requiring the student to dissect = skill-building mode. Use this to audit whether your course experience actually matches your positioning.
