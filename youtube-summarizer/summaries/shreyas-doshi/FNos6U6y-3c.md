# AI Won't Save You From Building the Wrong Thing

Video ID: `FNos6U6y-3c`

## Summary
This video challenges the assumption that AI-accelerated shipping solves the fundamental challenges of product management. The speaker argues that even with AI enabling faster development, most teams are not shipping 10x more features, and the core problem of deciding *what* to build remains unchanged. The central thesis is that solving any problem structurally creates new downstream problems, making indiscriminate building counterproductive. The talk is most relevant to product managers, founders, and engineering leaders navigating stakeholder pressure to ship more in the AI era.

## Key insights
- In practice, almost no teams are shipping 10x more features with AI — the speaker's audience poll showed fewer than 10% achieving even that, and essentially zero teams shipping 100x. Most are at 2–3x.
- Even at 2–3x velocity, teams still face the same prioritization problem: there are always more features they *could* build than they actually ship, so judgment about what to build remains the bottleneck.
- A key structural reality of product: choosing one feature often *precludes* another. The example given is comprehensive onboarding vs. light onboarding — once you commit to one, you effectively exclude the other (even A/B tests eventually converge on a winner).
- Solving a problem creates downstream problems. Picking comprehensive onboarding improved retention, but also reduced top-of-funnel conversion, increased user complaints, and generated customer support load. AI cannot fix problems that arise from structural product decisions.
- Feature accumulation has a hard limiting factor for human-facing products: user attention. More features do not equal a better product if users cannot absorb them. (The speaker notes AI agents may be an exception since they have effectively infinite attention.)
- The principle "solving a problem creates another problem" is presented as universal — the speaker invites the audience to verify it from their own careers. The correct response is not to avoid solving problems, but to consciously accept the tradeoffs that come with each solution.
- Stakeholder pressure to "use AI to solve everything" conflates shipping speed with product wisdom. Speed is real but is not the binding constraint.

## Use cases
- Product managers facing executive or stakeholder pressure to ship more features because "AI makes it easy now"
- Teams debating whether to expand their roadmap scope now that development velocity has increased
- PMs or founders deciding between mutually exclusive product directions (e.g., onboarding flows, pricing models, architectural approaches)
- Anyone experiencing unexpected downstream problems after shipping a feature that tested well
- Teams accumulating large feature sets and noticing user engagement or satisfaction declining despite more functionality
- Leaders trying to explain to non-technical stakeholders why "just build more with AI" is not a sound strategy

## Patterns & frameworks

**Solving a problem creates another problem**
The core mental model: every product decision that resolves one issue introduces new structural tradeoffs or downstream problems. It is not a failure of execution — it is inherent to the nature of building. The right response is to consciously choose *which* problems you are willing to live with, not to seek a solution that eliminates all problems.

**Feature preclusion / structural exclusion**
When you choose Feature A over Feature B, you do not just delay B — you often make B structurally impossible or irrelevant (at least without significant rework). The onboarding example illustrates this: a dynamic flow that tries to have both ends up being a third, more complex feature, not a free combination of the two.

**The attention ceiling**
For human users, attention is finite and is the true limiting agent on feature value. Shipping 100 extra features per year does not compound linearly into 100x more value — at some point additional features create noise, complexity, and confusion. This ceiling does not apply (or applies differently) when building for AI agents rather than humans.

**Velocity ≠ wisdom (AI shipping paradox)**
AI genuinely accelerates shipping, but the harder and more persistent challenge in product management is choosing *what* to ship. The speaker frames this as: AI solves the "how fast" problem but does nothing to solve the "which problem to solve" problem, which remains a human judgment exercise dependent on research, data, customer input, and competitive analysis.
