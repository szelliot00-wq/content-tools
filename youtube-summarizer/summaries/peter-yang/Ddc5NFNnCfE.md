# My Mom Has Cancer Again. Here’s How I’m Using AI to Fight It

Video ID: `Ddc5NFNnCfE`

## Summary
The creator shares his personal experience as a caregiver for his mother, who is facing cancer for the fourth time, and walks through a five-step AI-assisted framework he built to help patients and caregivers navigate the overwhelming medical, administrative, and emotional challenges of a cancer diagnosis. He created a free open-source ChatGPT skill called "F Cancer" that organizes medical information, generates doctor questions, and helps research treatment options. The video blends practical AI tooling with a personal reflection on the value of time with loved ones. It is most relevant to cancer patients, their caregivers, and anyone who wants to use AI for high-stakes personal healthcare navigation.

## Key insights
- Cancer affects 2 in 5 Americans at some point; when caregivers are included, almost no family goes untouched. Cancer death rates have fallen 35% since 1991, but 81% of cases still occur in people 55+.
- The creator built a free, open-source ChatGPT skill called "F Cancer" (hosted on GitHub at github.com/petergyang/fcancer) that any patient or caregiver can install to manage their loved one's case.
- The one-page brief in Google Docs is the cornerstone tool — it contains patient info, top 3 next actions/questions, known diagnosis facts, plain-English explanations of medical terms, and a dated care log.
- Medical records come in three distinct types that must be requested separately and proactively: written records (pathology reports, treatment history), imaging files (CT/PET scans in DICOM format, delivered on USB or CD-ROM), and pathology blocks/slides from biopsies — the last of these is the most valuable for tumor sequencing and second opinions, and can take the longest to retrieve (sometimes from offsite warehouses).
- AI is more useful for managing paperwork and follow-ups than for pure research — the creator found this surprising compared to his initial expectations.
- The cancer system is frustratingly slow and bureaucratic: two months passed between his mom's first CT scan and a treatment decision, with communication via fax and hand-delivered CDs — a stark contrast to the AI breakthroughs hyped on social media.
- Standard care in the US follows NCCN guidelines; patients can read these with a free account and use AI to summarize applicable options for their specific diagnosis and biomarkers.
- Getting a second opinion at a major cancer center (MD Anderson, MSK, Dana-Farber, Stanford, UCSF) is strongly recommended and can itself take up to a month, making early preparation of records critical.
- Tumor sequencing (e.g., via Boston Gene at ~$4,000) produces a biomarker report covering DNA, RNA, and other markers that can unlock more targeted treatment options beyond standard care.
- Clinical trials are worth researching via clinicaltrials.gov, but caution is warranted — Phase 1 and 2 trials are unproven and could potentially harm rather than help.
- Building a "board of experts" — oncologists, foundations, and informed individuals who reach out after the story is shared publicly — provides invaluable guidance; the creator BCC's this group on AI-drafted email updates.
- Sharing a loved one's cancer journey publicly or with a wider circle tends to surface unexpected help from people who have been through it themselves.
- Time with family is the video's closing theme: the creator argues that time with loved ones is the easiest resource to neglect because there is no explicit deadline or quantifiable cost, unlike money or career milestones.

## Use cases
- A family member newly appointed as caregiver for a cancer patient who needs to quickly get organized and ask the right questions at medical appointments.
- A patient or caregiver trying to consolidate scattered records from multiple hospitals into a single, AI-readable source of truth.
- Anyone preparing for a specialist appointment and wanting to generate the most high-value questions from existing medical context.
- A caregiver trying to navigate second-opinion requests at major cancer centers and unsure what records to gather or how to request them.
- Someone researching whether tumor sequencing or clinical trials are relevant to their loved one's specific diagnosis and biomarkers.
- A patient or caregiver who wants to keep a distributed group of medical advisors and supporters informed with concise, AI-drafted updates.
- Anyone in a "sandwich generation" situation (young children + aging parents) reflecting on how to allocate time more intentionally.

## Patterns & frameworks

**The Five-Step Cancer Caregiver Framework**
A repeatable process the creator follows with AI assistance:
1. Create a one-page brief (patient info, next actions, known facts, medical glossary, care log) in Google Docs as a living source of truth.
2. Organize all medical records (written records, imaging/DICOM files, pathology blocks) in a shared Google Drive, requested proactively and early.
3. Research treatment options in parallel with the diagnostic process — standard care via NCCN guidelines, second opinions at major cancer centers, tumor sequencing, and clinical trial research via clinicaltrials.gov.
4. Build a board of experts by sharing the journey publicly and maintaining them with regular AI-drafted briefing emails.
5. Protect quality time with the patient and family — treat it as the highest-value, most time-limited resource.

**The "Brief as Source of Truth" Pattern**
A single, shared Google Doc that all AI queries are grounded in. Rather than asking AI generic questions, every query is contextualized by the brief and the Google Drive of records, producing personalized rather than generic medical guidance.

**The "Board of Experts" Pattern**
Proactively recruit oncologists, patient advocates, and informed individuals who surface through public sharing. Maintain them with periodic BCC'd email updates drafted by AI, turning informal supporters into an advisory network.

**"Start the Request Process Early" Heuristic**
Since medical records (especially pathology blocks and DICOM imaging) take weeks to obtain, the framework treats record-gathering as a parallel workstream to begin immediately upon diagnosis — not something to handle after a treatment plan is set.
